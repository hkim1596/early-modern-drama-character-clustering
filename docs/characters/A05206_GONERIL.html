I marvel, Ragan, how you can endure To see that proud pert Peat, our youngest sister, So slightly to account of us, her elders, As if we were no better then her self! We cannot have a quaint device so soon, Or new made fashion, of our choice invention; But if she Like it, she will have the same, Or study newer to exceed us both. Beside, she is so nice and so demure; So sober, courteous, modest, and precise, That all the Court hath work enough to do, To talk how she exceeds me and you. By my virginity, rather then she shall have A husband before me, I'll marry one or other in his shirt: And yet I have made half a grant already Of my good will unto the King of Cornwall. What ever thouspeakst to us, kind Skalliger, Think that thou speakest it only to thy self. Thanks, gentle Skalliger, thy kindness undeserved, Shall not be unrequited, if we live. Nay, our revenge we will inflict on her, Shall be accounted piety in us: I will so flatter with my doting father, As he was never so flattered in his life. Nay, I will say, that if it be his pleasure, To match me to a beggar, I will yield: For why, I know what ever I do say, He means to match me with the Cornwall King. I smile to think, in what a woeful plighted Cordella will be, when we answer thus: For she will rather die, then give consent To join in marriage with the Irish King: So will our father think, she loves him not, Because she will not grant to his desire, Which we will aggravate in such bitter terms, That he will soon convert his love to hate: For he, you know, is always in extremes. Our royal Lord and father, in all duty, We come to know the tenor of your will, Why you so hastily have sent for us? I hope, my gracious father makes no doubt Of any of his daughters love to him: Yet for my partly, to show my zeal to you, Which cannot be in windy words rehearsed, I prize my love to you at such a rate, I think my life inferior to my love. Should you enjoin me for to tie a millstone About my neck, and leap into the Sea, At your command I willingly would do it: Yea, for to do you good, I would ascend The highest Turret in all Brittany, And from the top leap headlong to the ground: Nay, more, should you appoint me for to marry The meanest vassayle in the spacious world, Without reply I would accomplish it: In brief, command what ever you desire, And if I fail, no favour I require. Here is and answer answerless indeed: Were you my daughter, I should scarcely brook it. You were not best say I flatter: if you do, My deeds shall show, I flatter not with you. I love my father better then thou canst. I ever thought that pride would have a fallen. Sister, when did you see Cordella last, That pretty piece, that thinks none good enough To speak to her, because (sir-reverence) She hath a little beauty extraordinary? I have incensed my father so against her, As he will never be reclaimed again. Faith, sister, what moves you to bear her such good will? As welcome, as Leander was to Hero, Or brave Aeneas to the Carthage Queen: So and more welcome is your Grace to me. I prithee, Skalliger, tell me what thou thinkest: Could any woman of our dignity Endure such quips and peremptory taunts, As I do daily from my doting father? Doth't not suffice that I him keep of alms, Who is not able for to keep himself? But as if he were our better, he should think To check and snap me up at every word. I cannot make me a new fashioned gown, And set it forth with more then common cost; But his old doting doltish withered wit, Is sure to give a sense less check for it. I cannot make a banquet extraordinary, To grace my self, and spread my name abroad, But he, old fool, is captious by and by, And say, the cost would well suffice for twice. I prithee, Skalliger, tell me, if thou know, By any means to rid me of this woe. What, do you fear, that I have angered him? These things (although it be not so) Yet thou must affirm them to be true, With oaths and protestations as will serve, To driven my sister out of love with him, And cause my will accomplished to be. Then that they should be reconcilde again. They lie, that say, we sought our father's death. Peace (Puritan) dissembling hypocrite, Which art so good, that thou wilt prove stark nought: Anon, when as I have you in my fingers, I'll make you wish your self in Purgatory. Peace, peace, old fellow, thou art sleepy still. Well, Skalliger, for thy kind advice herein, I will not be ungrateful, if I live: I have restrained half his portion already, And I will presently restrain the other, That having no means to relieve himself, He may go seek elsewhere for better help.

Plot summary

Plot — The True Chronicle of King Leir (King Lear and his Three Daughters)
King Lear deems it a good moment to marry off his three daughters: with his wife recently dead, they no longer have a mother to direct them. His plan is to retire and divide the kingdom between them as their dowries, and to marry them to neighbouring kings in order to forestall foreign aggression. Cordella, however, is determined to marry for love rather than for diplomatic convenience, so Lear plans to circumvent her by laying on a test: the daughters will make a public declaration of how much they love their father, and he will then ask Cordella to show that love by complying with his wishes over her marriage. Scaliger warns the other daughters, Goneril and Regan, of what Lear has in mind, and misleads them into believing that their shares of the kingdom will reflect the extent of their declared love. Jealous of their younger sister’s beauty and renown, they flatter Lear so that Cordella’s simple statement of due filial love pales in comparison. Lear gives the kingdom wholly to them, summons the Kings of Cornwall and Cambria to be their husbands, and assigns them their shares by lot. Against the advice of the good counsellor Perillus, Lear banishes Cordella from court, and she plans to make her living working as a seamstress. By chance, she meets the King of France, who has come to Britain disguised in search of a wife, and he takes her back to France as his Queen. Lear goes to live with Cornwall and Goneril, but annoys her with his criticisms of her extravagance. Scaliger advises her that halving his allowance will persuade him to take himself off to Regan, and so indeed it does. Cornwall sends a messenger to Regan asking for news of Lear, but Goneril bribes him to deliver instead a libellous letter about her father’s behaviour; she also encourages him to murder Lear. Regan has the same idea, and sets up an opportunity, but at the last moment the messenger is struck by conscience, admits the truth to his intended victim: and runs away.
