I wish your Lordship's safety Which while I pray for, I must not forget To urge again the ways to fix you where No danger has access to threaten you. But fortified with new and pregnant reasons, That you should leave the Court. You dare be undone then. Would thou we to less degraded from thy titles, And swelling offices, that will ith' end Engulf thee passed a rescue, I had not come So far to trouble you at this time, but that I do not Like the loud tongues o'the world, That say the King has taken another favourite, The Constable a gay man, and a great, With a hugh train of faction too, the Queen, Chancellor, Treasurer, Secretary, and And army of state warriors whose discipline Is sure, and subtle to confusion, I hope the rumour's false, thou art so calm. It has not, And you are pleased, then you do mean to mix With unjust courses, the great Constable And you combining, that no suit may pass One of the grapples of your either's rape, I that abhorred, must I now entertain A thought, that your so straight, and simple custom To render Justice, and the common good, Should now be patched with policy, and wrested From the ingenious step you took, And hang Upon the shoulders of your enemy To bear you out in what you shame to act It follows then that both the acts must bear Like reconcilement, and if he will now Malign and malice you for crossing him Or any of his faction in their suits, Being now atoned, you must be one in all, One in corruption, and betwixt you two millstones New picked, and put together, must the grain Of good men's needful means to live, be ground Into your choking superfluities; You both too rich, they ruinde. I doubt it While enforced show perhaps, which will I fear Prove in true substance but a Millers whiteness, More sticking in your clothes then conscience. As if the total sum of favorites frailties Affected not the full rule of their Kings In their own partially disposed ambitions, And that Kings do no hazard infinitely In their free realties of rights and honours, Where they leave much for favourites powers to order. No more, now I must tell you daughter Least you forget your self, she is the Queen, And it becomes not you to vie with her Passion for passion, if your Lord stand fast To the full search of Law, Heaven will revenge him, And give him up precious to good men's loves If you attempt by these unruly ways To vindicate his justice, I me against you, Dear as I wish your husbands life and fame, Suffer are bound to suffer, not contest With Princes, since their Will and Acts must be Accounted one day to a Judge supreme.

Plot summary

Plot — Chabot, Admiral of France
Admiral Philip Chabot has been supplanted at court by a new royal favourite, the Duke Montmorency. The King intervenes to put a stop to their rivalry, but Montmorency is encouraged by his fellow courtiers to renege and seek Chabot’s downfall. The opportunity arises when Montmorency proffers a suit, concerning a merchant ship seized by the Spanish, and has the bill approved by the King and Council; it is forwarded to Chabot for signature, but he insists on reading it and, finding it unjust, tears it up. The Queen encourages her husband to treat Chabot’s action as criminal defiance, but the King claims he was only testing the Admiral’s probity, and expected him to destroy the bill. He sees Chabot privately and tries to persuade him to relent and allow the bill, but Chabot is adamant that no injustice must be done in the King’s name. Offended by his stubbornness, the King asks Poyet, the Lord Chancellor, to prepare the case against him. His secretary Allegre is racked, but confesses nothing against him. At his trial, the inferior judges point out that the only crimes mentioned in the accusation are trivial ones, but Poyet is adamant that Chabot must be convicted of high treason, and the judges sign the condemnation under duress. Meanwhile, the Queen meets Chabot’s wife and, impressed by her loyalty and humility, has a change of heart. She and Montmorency approach the King to plead for Chabot, but he says he cannot interfere with the workings of the law. The news of the conviction comes through, and Poyet presses for an early execution. The King summons Chabot and publicly pardons him, but Chabot insists that he has committed no crime and so needs no pardon. The King takes this for contempt, but asks to see the bill of condemnation. Sure enough, no specific crimes are listed, and Poyet’s over-zealous behaviour comes to light. The King has him arrested and put to trial: he is vilified by the same prosecutor who flattered him at Chabot’s arraignment. He is fined, deprived of office, and sentenced to perpetual imprisonment in his own castle. The King cancels the sentence, thinking it too lenient, and gives Chabot the right to substitute an alternative, heavier punishment. Chabot asks instead that Poyet be forgiven, then drops dead, exhausted by his experiences. The King complies with his dying wishes, and promises to look after his family.
