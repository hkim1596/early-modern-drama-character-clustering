Full speech

I thank his Grace, and kiss his hanes. I have another, but a worse; I am ashamed, it is a business.— I am bound to your nobleness. Sir, you shall know I have lost a foolish daughter, And with her all my patience; pilferd away, By a mean Captain of your Kings. I grant all that too: but this wretched fellow Reaches no further then the empty name, That serves to feed him; were he valiant, Or had but in him any noble nature, That might hereafter promise him a good man; My carcs were something lighter, and my graven A span yet from me. 'Tis such a scurvy name as Bessus, and now I think 'tis he. My daughter is a Whore, I feel it now too sensible; yet I will see her, Discharge my self of being Father to her, And then back to my Country, and there die; Farewell Captain. There he is indeed, And with him my disloyal child. Health to your Majesty. Several Businesses. My public business will appear by this: I have a message to deliver, which If it please you so to authorise, is And embassage from the Armenian state; Unto Arbaces for your liberty The offer's there set down, please you to read it. None Sir, all is as it was. All very well. Madam I should have seen you. Forgive you, why I am no kin to you, am I? Thou couldst prate unhappily Ere thou couldst go, would thou couldst do as well. And how does your custom hold out here. Are you in private still, or how? If thou couldst not say so much for thy self thou shouldst be Carted. Well Sir I will: but I have private business with you. How has my age deserved so ill of you, That you can pick no strumpets in the Land, But out of my breed. Yes, and I wish to have you know, I scorn To get a Whore for any Prince alive, And yet scorn will not help me thinks: My daughter Might have been spared, there were enough beside. Why is she with you then? Can she wait on you better then your men, Has she a gift in plucking off your stockings, Can she make Cawdles well, or cut your Corns, Why do you keep her with you? For your Queen I know you do contemn her, so should I And every Subject else think much at it. Then have I made a fair hand, I called her Whore, If I shall speak now as her Father, I cannot of use But greatly rejoice that she shall be a Queen: but if I should speak to you as a Statesman she were more fit To be your Whore. Yes Sir, I will go. And shall she be a Queen, she had more wit Then her old Father when she ran away: Shall she be a Queen, now by my troth 'tis fine, He dance out of all measure at her wedding: Shall I not Sir? I'll make these withered Kexes bear my body Two h rest gether above ground. Good God preserve you, you are and excellent King. Farewell sweet virtuous Daughter; Ineuer was so Joyful in my life, That I remember: shall she be a Queen? Now I perceive a man may weep for joy, I had thought they had lied that said so. We must have our King again, and will. 'Tis Spaconia. She is my daughter. Why I shall be made For ever, they belied this King with us And saved he was unkind. Do you take money? are you come to fell sin yet? perhaps I can help you to liberal Clients: or has not the King cast you off yet? Oh thou vild creature, whose best come mendation is, that thou art a young Whore. I would thy Mother had lived to see this: or rather would I had died ere I had seen it: why didst not make me acquainted when thou were first resolved to be a Who e? I would have seen thy hight lust satisfied more privately. I would have kept a dancer, and a whole consort of Musicians in mine own house, only to fiddle thee.

Plot summary

Plot — A King and No King
Arbaces has been away from his kingdom for many years fighting a war with Armenia, which he has just won. He has received reports from his Lord Protector, Gobrias, about the virtue and accomplishments of his younger sister Panthea, whom he has not seen since she was nine years old. He offers her hand in marriage to Tigranes, the vanquished Armenian King, but Tigranes demurs. Arbaces takes him back to Iberia to see her for himself. Tigranes’ objection lies in the fact that he already has a girlfriend, Spaconia, and he bribes the cowardly Bessus to bring her along and place her in Panthea’s household. Once there, she begs Panthea not to marry Tigranes, and Panthea agrees. Arane, Arbaces’ mother, has been trying to assassinate him since he came to the throne. Another attempt is foiled, but he pardons her when he returns in triumph. She clearly has an ulterior motive which is known to Gobrias, but both are discreet about its precise nature. Arbaces’ first meeting with Panthea as an adult proves unsettling: he falls in love with her. So does Tigranes. Identifying a rival, Arbaces imprisons him. Torn between desire and conscience, he also imprisons Panthea. Panthea gives Spaconia a token authorizing her to visit Tigranes in prison. He rejects her, but Arbaces finds them together and assumes that she is a go-between. Gobrias importunes him to release Panthea, who persuades him that his suspicions are unfounded: she loves him, and would act upon that love if to do so were not incestuous. The Armenians send Spaconia’s father as an ambassador to negotiate for Tigranes’ release. He believes his daughter has eloped with Bessus, but his denunciations of her bring Tigranes to his senses: he will make Spaconia his Queen. When Arbaces decides to kill Gobrias for corrupting him to incest, the truth comes out. Arbaces is not of royal blood. The old King married Arane when he was old and impotent, and, fearful about the succession, Gobrias and Arane conspired to produce an heir: Gobrias gave Arane his new-born son, and she faked his birth. Later, it proved that the King was not so impotent after all, and he died leaving Arane pregnant with Panthea, but by the time this was known, the young Arbaces had already succeeded to the throne. Since then, Gobrias and Arane have both been trying to put matters to rights, Arane with her murder plots and Gobrias by encouraging Arbaces to marry his putative sister and so establish the legitimacy of his teign.
