Full speech

That Grecian (Sir) is at the Court arrived, Whole wisdom fame through all the world records. And yet what wonder though he wander thus, Whom still by success treacherous fortune blinds? Though this indeed seem somewhat strange to us, Who have with learning purified our minds; Was he not born heir of a mighty state? And used with Fortune's smiles, not feared for frowns, Does measure all things by his own conceit? A great defect, which fatal is to Crowns; Then from his youth still trusting in a Throne, With all that pride could crave, or wealth could give, Used with entreaties, and controlled by none, He would the tongue of liberty deprive; Though to his sighed I dare not thus appear, Whose partial judgement, far from reason parts; I grieve to see your entertainment here So far inferior to your own deserts; That matchless wisdom which the world admires, And (ravished with delight,) amazed hears, Since not in consort with his vain desires, Did seem impleasant to distempered ears: Ears which can entry give to no discourse, Save that which enters fraughted with his praise; He can love none but them that love his course, And thinks all fools who use no flattering phrase: This with the great ones does the gods displease, Though spreading all her heavenly treasures forth, They (if not in their livery them to please) Do virtue vilify, as of no worth. Who come to Court, must with Kings faults comport. A wise man at their imperfections winks. So should you loose your self, and them not save. By this you should their indignation find. It would be long ere you were thus preferred. They guerdon as they love, they love by guess. It's good to be still by the Prince approved. But by this mean, all hope of honour fails. I think they should excel (for virtue rare) All men in wit, who unto men give laws; Kings of their kingdoms, as the centers are, To which each weighty thing by nature draws: For as the mighty rivers, little streams, And all the liquid powers which rise or fallen, Do seek in sundry parts by several seams, The Ocean's bosom that receives them all. It as a Steward of the tumid deeps Does send them back by many secret veins, And (as the earth hath needs of moisture) keeps These humid treasures to refresh the Plains. Thus are Kings breasts the depths where daily flow Clear streams of knowledge with rare treasures charged, So that continually their wisdoms grow, By many helps, which others want, enlarged: For those who have intelligence over all, Do commonly communicate to Kings, All th'accidents of weight that chance to fallen; Which great advantage, Greatness to them brings. They (jealously disposed) comment on minds, And these who Arts, or Nature's gifts enhance, (Whose valve no where else a Merchant finds ) Do come to Kings, as who may them advance. No doubt great Jove, since they supply his place, (So with their charge to make their virtues even) Does give to them some supernatural grace, Vice-gods on earth, great Lieutenants ot heaven. It easy is another's faults to spy, And paint in th'aire the shadows of our minds, Whilst apprehending with the inward eye A high perfection which no practice finds. If you at Court to credit would arise, You must not seek by truth to gain renown, But sometime must applaud what you despise, And smile in show whilst in effect you frown. In all the parts where he hath chanced to be, In foreign bounds, or where he first saw light, He never did such stately wonders see, As since this Court enriched hath his sighed; When regal shows had ravished first his eye, As mountains nurslings, little simple swains, Who used with infant floods them never spy Sport, portative (Like Serpents) through the Plains, When one of them first comes to view the vails, And wanton water-Nymphs there (wondering) sees, The rareness of the sighed so much prevails, That rillets rivers seem, the rivers Seas: So all the guards that garnished Solons way, Did to his mind a great amazement bring, The gallants (golden statues) made him stay; Each Groom a Prince, each Esquire seemed a King, And now he comes to gain your long'd-for sighed; Whom in his mind no doubt he does adore, He gazed on those who held of you their light, Sun of this soil, he must admire you more; Now he over all will spread your praises forth, A famous witness of your glorious reign: "The record of one wise man is more worth, "Then what a world of others would maintain.

Plot — Croesus Cw
Solon visits Croesus, hoping to persuade him to give up his immoderate love of gold and think about less transitory things. He explains that all mortality is subject to change, and tells Croesus stories about the importance of posthumous fame, but Croesus considers them irrelevant to his situation as a king. Solon realizes that Croesus wili change only by losing the power which has made him arrogant. Adrastus is banished from Phrygia after accidentally killing his brother. (His intended victim was the man who poisoned his girlfriend.) Croesus has a nightmare about the death of his son Atys, and becomes excessively protective: he makes Atys marry in order to secure the succession, appoints him a bodyguard, and bans the type of sharp implement which caused his death in the dream. Mysia is ravaged by a wild boar, and its people appeal to Croesus for help. He is happy to hear that they don’t want his money, but distressed that they ask instead that he send Atys to hunt the boar. Atys protests at his father’s reluctance, so Croesus accedes, assigning Adrastus to look after him during the mission. Atys is killed, and Adrastus kills himself in remorse. In the past, Astyages, King of Media and Croesus’ brother-in-law, was perturbed by a prophecy that he would be deposed by his grandson. When the child was born, he tried to have it killed; but the herdsman who was given the job spared its life and adopted it as a replacement for his own stillborn baby. During his youth, Cyrus’ princely behaviour led to his identification. Astyages served Harpagus a banquet of his own son’s flesh, so Harpagus contacted Cyrus and raised a rebellion. Now Cyrus has led the Persian army to depose Astyages. Against his counsellor’s advice, Croesus plans to intervene and conquer Persia, but Cyrus anticipates him and attacks pre-emptively, ordering that Croesus be taken alive. Croesus is nearly killed by a Persian soldier, but saved when his dumb son cries out and identifies him. Cyrus intends to burn him at the stake, but is impressed by Croesus’ acknowledgement that Solon was right to warn him not to trust in worldly things. Cyrus frees him, and orders restraint in his soldiers’ looting.
