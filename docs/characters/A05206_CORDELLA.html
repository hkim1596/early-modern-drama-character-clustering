Oh, how I do abhor this flattery! I cannot paint my duty forth in words, I hope my deeds shall make report for me: But look what love the child does owe the father, The same to you I bear, my gracious Lord. The praise were great, spoke from another's mouth: But it should seem your neighbours dwell far off. What then is left for his third daughters dowry, Lovely Cordella, whom the world admires? Ah Pilgrim's, what avails to show the cause, When there's no means to find a remedy? To touch a soar, does aggravate the pain. Kind Palmer, which so much defir'st to hear The tragic tale of my unhappy youth: Know this in brief, I am the hapless daughter Of Leir, sometime King of Btittany. O, do not mock with those in misery, Nor do not think, though fortune have the power, To spoil mine honour, and debase my state, That she hath any interest in my mind: For if the greatest Monarch on the earth, Should sue to me in this extremity, Except my heart could love, and heart could Like, Better then any that I ever saw, His great estate no more should move my mind, Then mountains move by blast of every wind. The Like to thee did never these eyes behold; O live to add new torments to my grief: Why didst thou thus entrap me unawares? Ah Palmer, my estate does not befit A kingly marriage, as the case now stands. Whilom when as I lived in honours height, A Prince perhaps might postulate my love: Now misery, dishonour and disgrace, Hath light on me, and quite reversed the case. Thy King will hold thee wise, if thou surcease The suit, whereas no dowry will ensue. Then be advised, Palmer, what to do: Cease for thy King, seek for thy self to woo. My mind is low enough to love a Palmer, Rather then any King upon the earth. Oh yes, I can, and happy if I might: I'll hold thy Palmer's staff within my hand, And think it is the Sceptre of a Queen. Sometime isle set thy Bonnet on my head, And think I wear a rich imperial Crown. Sometime isle help thee in thy holy prayers, And think I am with thee in Paradise. I never in my life took more delight In any journey, then I do in this: Ic did me good, when as we happened to light Amongst the merry crew of country folk, To see what industry and pains they took, To win them commendations amongst their friends. Plot — The True Chronicle of King Leir (King Lear and his Three Daughters)
King Lear deems it a good moment to marry off his three daughters: with his wife recently dead, they no longer have a mother to direct them. His plan is to retire and divide the kingdom between them as their dowries, and to marry them to neighbouring kings in order to forestall foreign aggression. Cordella, however, is determined to marry for love rather than for diplomatic convenience, so Lear plans to circumvent her by laying on a test: the daughters will make a public declaration of how much they love their father, and he will then ask Cordella to show that love by complying with his wishes over her marriage. Scaliger warns the other daughters, Goneril and Regan, of what Lear has in mind, and misleads them into believing that their shares of the kingdom will reflect the extent of their declared love. Jealous of their younger sister’s beauty and renown, they flatter Lear so that Cordella’s simple statement of due filial love pales in comparison. Lear gives the kingdom wholly to them, summons the Kings of Cornwall and Cambria to be their husbands, and assigns them their shares by lot. Against the advice of the good counsellor Perillus, Lear banishes Cordella from court, and she plans to make her living working as a seamstress. By chance, she meets the King of France, who has come to Britain disguised in search of a wife, and he takes her back to France as his Queen. Lear goes to live with Cornwall and Goneril, but annoys her with his criticisms of her extravagance. Scaliger advises her that halving his allowance will persuade him to take himself off to Regan, and so indeed it does. Cornwall sends a messenger to Regan asking for news of Lear, but Goneril bribes him to deliver instead a libellous letter about her father’s behaviour; she also encourages him to murder Lear. Regan has the same idea, and sets up an opportunity, but at the last moment the messenger is struck by conscience, admits the truth to his intended victim: and runs away.
