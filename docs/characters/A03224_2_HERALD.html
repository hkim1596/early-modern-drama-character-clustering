I go my Liege resolved to your High will. The King my master recks not Burgundy, And scorns S. Paul that treacherous Constable, His puissance is sufficient in it self, To conquer France Like his progenitors. So please it your most sacred Majesty, To send unto my gram jous Sovereign, Equal conditions for the bonds of peace, And restitution of his injuries, His temper is not of obdurate malice, But sweet relenting princely clemency, Perform your promise of a thousand crowns, And second me with some fit messenger, And I will undertake to work your peace. Health and crease of honour wait on Edward. First it is covenanted that Lewis King of France, ac cording to the custom of his predecessors, shall do homage to King Edward, King of England, as his Sovereign and true heir to all the Dominions of France. Secondly it is covenanted that Lewis King of France, shall pay unto Edward King of England, immediately upon the agreement betwixt their Majesties, seventy five thou sand crowns of the sun, towards the charge King Edward hath been at since his arrival in these parts of France. Thirdly and lastly, it is covenanted, that over and be side those seventy five thousand crowns of the sun, now presently to be paid, Lewis King of France shall yearly here after, during the life of Edward King of England, pay fifty thousand crowns more without fraud or guile, to be ten dreaded at his Majesties Castle, commonly called the Tower of London. Swear on this book King Lewis so help you God, You mean no otherwise then you have said. My Sovereign.

Plot summary

Plot — 2 Edward the Fourth
King Edward invades France; but the Duke of Burgundy and the Constable of France fail to supply the military aid they promised. Several English soldiers are shot by the Constable's men in a failed attempt to assassinate Edward; the Constable pleads an accident,The English herald sent to King Louis is offered peace terms, which Edward modifies: Louis must submit to his suzerainty, and pay both the expenses of the invasion and an annual tribute thereafter. Louis accepts the offer and a treaty is agreed. Burgundy and the Constable try to double-cross both Edward and one another: each writes independently to both Edward and Louis, offering Louis Edward's murder and Edward assistance against Louis. Edward feigns deafness, forcing the envoys to speak loud enough for the hidden Louis to hear; then confronts the envoys with their masters' treachery. He returns to England, leaving troops in France to help Louis rout the traitors. Jane Shore visits the London prisons, distributing alms to the prisoners. Brakenbury solicits her help to procure a pardon for his kinsman Strangwidge, whose actions against French shipping were committed in ignorance of the recent treaty which made them piratical. Also condemned is his passenger, known as Flood but actually Matthew Shore. The Queen first confronts, then forgives Jane for her adultery with Edward. The King pardons Strangwidge and his crew, and Jane pays their prison fees herself. Brakenbury employs 'Flood' at the Tower. Edward has fallen ill and is anxious about a prophecy that 'G' will dispose of his sons and usurp the throne. His brother Clarence is imprisoned and drowned in the Tower. Edward dies and Richard of Gloucester becomes Lord Protector. Jane is expelled from court, and goes to stay with Mistress Blague. Richard lodges the young King and his brother in the Tower and has them murdered. 'Flood' is wounded in an altercation with the murderers and seeks succour at Mistress Blague's inn, where he is tended by Jane. The ghost of Friar Anselm tells Dr Shaw that everyone involved in the death of the princes will come to a bad end; Richard will reign only three years. Richard issues a proclamation banning anyone from giving Jane food or shelter, and Mistress Blague turns her out; but not soon enough to evade being arrested. Jane does penance.
