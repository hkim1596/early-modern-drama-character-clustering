And God defend that any profane hand Should offer sacrilege to such a Saint. Lovely Isabella, by this duteous kiss, That draws partly of my Soul along with it, Had I but thought my rude intrusion Had waked the Dovelike spleen harboured within you, Life and my first born should not satisfy Such a transgression, worthy of a check, But that Immortals wink at my offence, Makes me presume more boldly: I am come To raise you from this so infernal sadness. Fairest, till now, Love was and Infant in my Oratory. I nee'r was so kissed, leave thus to please, Flames into flames, seas thou pourest into seas. In the Swans down, and tomb thee in mine arms. By heaven my love's as chaste as thou art fair, And both exceed comparison: by this kiss, That crowns me Monarch of another world Superior to the first, fair, thou shalt see As unto heaven, my love so unto thee. And as I sacrifice this to his Ghost, With this expire all corrupt thoughts of youth, That fame-insatiate Devil Jealousy, And all the sparks that may bring unto flame, Hate betwixt man and wife or breed defame. You shall not be my Orator (Lady) that pleads thus for your self. Give them kind entertainment. Some worthy friends of mine, my Lord, unknown to me, too lavish of their loves, Bring their own welcome in a solemn Masque. I could give it another interpretation. Me thinks this Lover has learned, of women, to deal by contraries: if so, then here he says, the Widow is his only hope. Gallants I thank you, and Begin a health to your Mistress's. Fit Ladies fit, you have had standing long. Wee'll after her. Gallants, unmasque I pray, And taste a homely banquet we entreat. Exit Rob. Carded. and Lights. The Gods send her no Horse, a poor old age. Eternal woe, and sickness lasting rage. Furies supply that place, for I will not: no, She that can forsake me when pleasure's in the full, Fresh and untired, what would she on the least barren coldness? I warrant you she has already God Her Bravoes, and her Ruffians: the meanest whore Will have one buckler, but your great ones more. The shores of Sicily retains not such a Monster, Though to Galley-slaves they daily prostitute. To let the Nuptial Tapers give light to her new lust, Who would have thought it? She that could no more forsake my company, Then can the day forsake the glorious presence of the Sun. When I was absent, then her galled eyes Would have shed April showers, and out-wept The clouds in that same overpassionate mood: When they drowned all the world, yet now forsakes me: Women your eyes shed glances Like the Sun: Now shines your brightness, now your light is done. On the sweetest Flowers you shine, it's but by chance, And on the basest Weed you'll waste a glance. Your beams once lost can never more be found: Unless we wait until your course run round, (And take you at fist hand.) Since I cannot Enjoy the noble title of a man, But after-ages, as our virtues are Buried while we are living, will found out My infamy, and her degenerate shame; Yet in my life I'll smother't if I may, And, Like a dead man, to the world bequeathe These houses of vanity, Mills, and Lands. Take what you will, I will not keep among you Servants, And welcome some religious Monastery, A true sworn Beadsman I'll hereafter be, And wake the morning cock with holy prayers. Dissuade me not, my will shall be my King; I thank thee Wife, a fair change thou hast given, I leave thy lust to woo the Love of Heaven. Exit cum servis. Lady: it seems your eye is still the same, Forgetful of what most it should behold, Do not you know me then? Madame. The worse my fate that plows a marble quarry: Primaleon yet thy Image was more kind, Although thy love not half so true as mine. Dance they that list, I sail against the wind. Bear record all, you blessed Saints in heaven, I come not to torment thee in thy death: For of himself he's terrible enough, But call to mind a Lady Like your self. And think how ill in such a beauteous soul, Upon the instant morrow of her nuptials, Apostasy and vild revolt would show: With all imagine that she had a Lord, Jealous, the Air should ravish her chaste looks: Doting Like the creator in his models, Who views them every minute, and with care, Mixed in his fear of their obedience to him. Suppose he sung through famous Italy, More common then the looser songs of Petrarch: To every several Zanies instrument, And he poor wretch, hoping some better sat, Might call her back from her Adulterate purpose: Lives in obscure, and almost unknown life, Till hearing, that she is condemned to die: For he once loved her, lends his pined corpse, Motion to bring him to her stage of honour Where drowned in woe: at her so dismal chance, He clasps her: thus he falls into a trance. Plot summary

Plot — The Insatiate Countess
Suitors visit Isabella, who is in open mourning for her dead husband, but secretly glad to be free of his jealousy and restrictions. She accepts Count Roberto’s suit and they are married the following day; but when she dances with Count Rogero in the wedding masque, her affections turn. She woos Rogero by letter and elopes with him to Pavia; Roberto enters holy orders. At Pavia, Rogero is visited by his friend Gniaca; Isabella falls in love with the newcomer, and feigns illness to give herself an opportunity to seduce him. After bedding Gniaca, she then feigns penitence to free herself from Rogero. The spurned lover takes his revenge by circulating bitter satires on Isabella’s promiscuity, so she sets on Gniaca to kill him. In the attempt, Gniaca is reconciled with his former friend, so Isabella seduces Don Sago, colonel in the Spanish power occupying Pavia, to murder both Rogero and Gniaca. Sago kills Rogero, and is arrested, but pardoned by the Duke of Medina. Isabella’s attempt to bribe the Duke fails to secure her freedom, and she is beheaded after a scaffold reconciliation with Roberto. In the sub-plot, the enemies Claridiana and Mizaldus are both married on the same day. They quarrel when they meet, and Mizaldus’s wife Abigail and Lady Lentulus, who are close friends, compare notes and swap places: each receives her own husband in the other’s bedroom. Meanwhile their friends Mendosa and Lady Lentulus, attracted to one another, try to circumvent the latter’s vow not to admit a man through her front door: he climbs up to her window by rope ladder, but falls into the street, where the watch find him injured and send him to a surgeon. Claridiana and Mizaldus are arrested in each other’s house; appalled at the thought of being cuckolded, they both confess to murdering Mendosa. Mendosa in turn tries to save Lady Lentulus’s honour with a false confession that he was attempting to burgle her house. In court, the Duke establishes that Claridiana and Mizaldus are telling incompatible stories about the ‘murder’, and they are sentenced to death in jest; in Mendosa’s case, however, the sentence is genuine. Claridiana and Mizaldus are determined to die until confronted with evidence of their wives’ chastity; with reconciliation comes reprieve.
