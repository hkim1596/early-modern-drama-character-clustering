Health to Caesar. Exeunt. Epa. & Euph. The Governor is stout, and does resolve To stand the extremest hazard of the war Before he yield Pelusium. Fergusiu the Philosopher Condemned to death by you. He craves a word with Arius ere he die. His own hand. In Cleopatra's arms By her with ropes let up into the tomb, After his deadly wound. How fares your Majesty? O Madam, Caesar's the unexampled mirror Of royalty, and does as far exceed All petie Kings in goodness as in power. And if my humblest services in ought May give content to royal Cleopatra I shall be proud to be commanded still. Will you command my service? Fear not Madam. A quick performance, it rejoices me To see her look so cheerily again. Exit Epa. Never more cheery Sir. Her looks express her Hope's; nor in her words Can she conceal her inward cheerfulness. But one thing, Sir, she said she had forgotten, Which nearly did concern both you and her; And that in such a cause she durst not trust A common messenger, requesting me To give it to your hands. Here lies her servant bleeding, Not dead: speak Charmio, how died the Queen? Two of Antonius truest servants, Caesar, Who bring a letter from their dying Lord. Caesar. they whisp r. Slave, my Lord.

Plot summary

Plot — Cleopatra, Queen of Egypt
PLOT Though he has a wife in Rome, Antonius is devoted to Cleopatra. Provocatively, he bestows on her three eastern Provinces which were under Roman rule. News arrives from Rome that all but one of his rivals have been eclipsed; the remaining politician, Caesar, is too proud to share power and has engrossed the others' confiscated estates to himself rather than dividing them with Antonius. Antonius abandons hedonism to prepare for war, and Cleopatra forces him to allow her to accompany him. Portents appear in Egypt and Rome, including a comet. During the decisive battle, Cleopatra flees and Antonius follows her, leaving his navy to be massacred; his soldiers transfer their allegiance to Caesar. Antonius goes to live in misanthropic seclusion, madly taking on the name and identity of Timon of Athens. Cleopatra offers Caesar gold to keep herself and her dynasty on the Egyptian throne. Caesar wants the gold, but also wants to display her at his triumph; but, knowing that she will never allow herself to be humiliated, he decides on a ruse. Meanwhile, Cleopatra investigates suicide methods, only to be told that there is no chemical poison which is both fast-acting and painless: her only option is the bite of an asp. She tests the claim on two condemned prisoners, administering the asp to one; when it does indeed appear to be a tranquil death, the other man is spared. Antonius is brought back to his senses by the news of his army's defection, and prepares to return to Cleopatra. While she is expecting his arrival, Thyreus brings her a message from Caesar, who claims to be in love with her. She is minded to accept and give Caesar one of her towns, but Antonius' friends suspect a plot and Thyreus is imprisoned. She accuses Antonius of unreasonable jealousy and threatens suicide, so Thyreus is released and sent back to Caesar with her acceptance of his terms. Cleopatra realizes that she has played false on both sides: her arrangement with Caesar will cost her Antonius' love, but conversely she cannot trust Caesar. While Antonius goes into battle, she retires to her tomb. After Antonius is routed, she sends him false news of her death, and he reacts suicidally. Learning the truth too late, he has himself taken to the tomb so that he can die in Cleopatra's arms. She realizes Caesar's insincerity and resolves to kill herself with the asp.
