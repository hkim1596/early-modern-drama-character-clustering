Full speech

No better man then Monsieur Mugeroune, Whose wit is sharp, whose eloquence is found, His presence gracious, and his courage good, A gentleman, a scholar, and a soldier, A complete man for such and Embassage: Art thou content to be employde Mugeroune, In this negotiation to King Edward? Nay rather whither goes the Constable? O excellent, I know that in thy soul, Thou knowest that I do purpose nothing less, Nay I do know for all thy outward show, Thou hast no meaning once to look on him. Brother dissembler, leave this colouring, With him that means as falsely as thy self. And for my better countenance in this case, My lingering siege at Nuse will serve the turn, There will I spend the time to disappoint, King Edwards hope of my consoyning with him. Ici sont mon secrets, beau temps pour moi. Are you so crafty Constable? proceed, proceed, You quick sharp sighted man, imagine me Blind, witless, and a filly Idiot, That pries not into all your policies, Who I? no, God does know my simple wit, Can never found a judgement of such reach, As is our cunning Constable of France: Persuade thy self so still, and when time serves, And that thou art in most extremity, Needing my help, then take thou heed of me, In mean while Sir, you are the only man That hath my heart, hath? I, and great reason too, Thus it befits men of deep reach to do. Well Constable, yowl back again to Nuse, And not aid English Edward? Am I not come according to my word? I make no question of it, yet the Constable Pressed with no such occasion as I was, Might have excused us both if he had pleased. So much he signified to me by letter. Perish my soul, King Edward, if I knew Of any such intention: yet I did, and grieve that it hath sped no otherwise. A peace concluded, sayest thou? is't not so? I have found it now, the villain Constable Hath secretly with Edward thus compact, To join our King and him in amity, And thereby doubtless God into his hands, Such lands and Dukedoms as I aimed at, And leaves me disappointed in my hope, A plague upon such crastie cosening, Now shall I be a mark for them to aim at, And that boil ane to triumph in my toil. Well fellow for thy pains take that, Leave me alone, for I am much displeasoe. Now Constable, this peace, this peace, What think you of it man? I think he that did contrive the same, Was little less then a dissembling villain. I, art thou good at that? Adieu Sir, I may chance to hit you pat. My forces lay before the city Nuse, From which I could not rise, but with dishonour, Unless upon some composition had.

Plot summary

Plot — 2 Edward the Fourth
King Edward invades France; but the Duke of Burgundy and the Constable of France fail to supply the military aid they promised. Several English soldiers are shot by the Constable's men in a failed attempt to assassinate Edward; the Constable pleads an accident,The English herald sent to King Louis is offered peace terms, which Edward modifies: Louis must submit to his suzerainty, and pay both the expenses of the invasion and an annual tribute thereafter. Louis accepts the offer and a treaty is agreed. Burgundy and the Constable try to double-cross both Edward and one another: each writes independently to both Edward and Louis, offering Louis Edward's murder and Edward assistance against Louis. Edward feigns deafness, forcing the envoys to speak loud enough for the hidden Louis to hear; then confronts the envoys with their masters' treachery. He returns to England, leaving troops in France to help Louis rout the traitors. Jane Shore visits the London prisons, distributing alms to the prisoners. Brakenbury solicits her help to procure a pardon for his kinsman Strangwidge, whose actions against French shipping were committed in ignorance of the recent treaty which made them piratical. Also condemned is his passenger, known as Flood but actually Matthew Shore. The Queen first confronts, then forgives Jane for her adultery with Edward. The King pardons Strangwidge and his crew, and Jane pays their prison fees herself. Brakenbury employs 'Flood' at the Tower. Edward has fallen ill and is anxious about a prophecy that 'G' will dispose of his sons and usurp the throne. His brother Clarence is imprisoned and drowned in the Tower. Edward dies and Richard of Gloucester becomes Lord Protector. Jane is expelled from court, and goes to stay with Mistress Blague. Richard lodges the young King and his brother in the Tower and has them murdered. 'Flood' is wounded in an altercation with the murderers and seeks succour at Mistress Blague's inn, where he is tended by Jane. The ghost of Friar Anselm tells Dr Shaw that everyone involved in the death of the princes will come to a bad end; Richard will reign only three years. Richard issues a proclamation banning anyone from giving Jane food or shelter, and Mistress Blague turns her out; but not soon enough to evade being arrested. Jane does penance.
