Full speech

What? what? my friends, will not this room receive? The Time? Lo I the man, that hate the time That is, that love it not; and (though in rhyme, I here do speak it) with this whip you see, Do lash the Time, and am myself lash-free. If you would see Time quake and shake, but name us, It is for that, we are both beloved, and famous. Then I will look on Time, and love the same, And drop my whip: who's this! my Mistress! Fame! The lady whom I honour, and adore! What luck had I not to see her before! Pardon me, Madam, more than most accursed, That did not spy your Ladyship at first, To-have given the stoop, and to salute the skirts Of her, to whom all Ladies else are flirts! It is for you, I revel so in rhyme, Dear Mistress, not for hope I have the Time Will grow the better by it. To serve Fame Is all my end, and get my self a name. Oh, you the Curious, Breath you to see a passage so injurious, Done with despite, and carried with such tumor against me, that am so much the friend of rumor? (I would say Fame? ) whose Muse hath rid in rapture On a soft ambling verse to every capture, From the strong guard, to the weak child that reads me, And wonder both of him that loves, or dread's me! Who with the lash of my immortal pen Have scourged all sorts of vices, and of men! Am I rewarded, thus? have I, I say, From Envies self torn praise, and bays away, With which my glorious front, and word at large, Triumphs in print at my admirers charge. When have I walked the streets, but happy he That had the finger first to point at me, Prentice, or Journeyman! The shop does know it! The unlettered Clarke! major and minor Poet! The Sempster hath sat still as I passed by, And dropped her needle! Fishwives stayed their cry The Boy with buttons, and the Basket wench! To vent their wares into my works do trench! A pudding-wife that would despise the Times, Hath uttered frequent pennyworths, through my rhymes, And, with them, dived into the Chambermaid, And she unto her Lady hath conveyed The seasoned morsels, who hath sent me pensions, To cherish, and to heighten my inventions. Well, Fame shall know it yet, I have my faction, And friends about me, though it please detraction, To do me this affront. Come forth that love me, And now, or never, spite of Fame, approve me.

Plot summary

Plot — The Masque of Augurs
Some citizens have heard rumours that the royal poet and architect have run out of ideas. Realizing that this may mean there will be no masque for the King’s entertainment, they come to the buttery at court to offer one of their own, featuring dancing bears. The artist Van Goose believes he can do better, and produces, on request, an antimasque of pilgrims, who dance until they are scared off by Apollo. Apollo descends and raises his mortal children from the dead. He has come to court to establish a.college of augurs, presided over by the King’s own son. The augurs arrive and dance. Their augury is interpreted as signifying Britain’s excellent prospects of peace, freedom from faction, and undisturbed royal power.
