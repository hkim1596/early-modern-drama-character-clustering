Your welcome was but duty, gentle Lord: For wherefore have we given us our wealth, But to make our betters welcome when they come? Oh, this goes hard when traitors must be flattered: But life is sweet, and I cannot withstand it. God (I hope) will revenge the quarrel of my King. I say, sir Gilbert, looking on my daughter, I curse the hour that ere I God the girl: For sir, she may have many wealthy suitors, And yet she disdains them all, to have Poor George a Green unto her husband. I warrant you, my Lord. Nay, are you not some crafty quean, That comes from George a Green, that rascal, With some letters to my daughter? I will have you searched. Why are you muffled? I Like you the worse For that. A pretty wench of smiling countenance, Old men can Like, although they cannot love, I, and love, though not so brief as young men can. Well, go in, my wench, and speak with my daughter. I wonder much at the Earl of Kendall, Being a mighty man, as still he is, Yet for to be a traitor to his king, Is more then God or man will well allow: But what a fool am I to talk of him? My mind is more here of the pretty lass: Had she brought some forty pounds to town. I could be content to make her my wife: Yet I have herd it in a proverb said, He that is old, and marries with a lass, Lies but at home, and proves himself and ass. How now, my wench, how is't? what not a word? Alas, poor soul, the toothache plagues her soar. Well, my wench, here is and Angel for to buy thee And I pray thee use mine house, The oftener the more welcome: farewell. I am, and it Like your majesty. Yes, my Lord, if he will let me marry With this lovely lass. Then do I give my daughter unto George. Marry, my Lord, I think this boy hath More knavery, than all the world beside. Yet am I content that George shall both have My daughter and my lands. My Lord, she is praying for George a Green: He is the man, and she will none but him.
