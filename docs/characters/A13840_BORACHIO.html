The same. For birth, growth, state, decay and death: Only, a Man's beholding to his Nature For better composition o'the two. That shows there's nothing in a Man, above His nature; if there were, considering t'is His beings excellency, would not yield To Nature's weakness. That revolution is too short me thinks. If this life comprehends our happiness, How foolish to desire to die so soon? And if our time runs home unto the length Of Nature, how improvident it were To spend our substance on a minutes pleasure, And after live and age in misery? Wealth is Lord Of all felicity. Both miserable and contemptible. Sir enough, I understand the mark whereat you aim. The substance of our former argument Was wealth. Young Charlemont is going to the war. Mark me then. Me thinks, the pregnant wit of Man, might make The happy absence of this Charlemont, A subject for commodious providence. He has a wealthy Father; ready even To drop into his graven. And no men power When Charlemont is gone, can interpose betwixt you and him. My resolution has already bound Me to your service. His own profession would report him pure. He is the man whom Castabella loves. Has left the passage of our practice free. I bespeak Employment in't. I'll be and instrument To grace performance with dexterity. As I vowed; Your instrument shall make your project proud. Yes. The enemy, defeated of a fair Advantage by a flattering stratagem; Plants all the Artillery against the Town. Whose thunder and lightning made our bulwarks shake; And threatened in that terrible report, The storm wherewith they meant to second it. Th'assault was general. But for the place That promised most advantage to beforced; The pride of all their Army was drawn forth, And equally divided into Front, And Rear. They marched. And coming to a stand, Ready to pass our Channel at and ebb, W advised it for our safest course, to draw Our vices up and mak't unpassable. Our Governor opposed; and suffered'em To charge us home even to the Ramp ers foot. But when their front was forcing up our breach, At push o'pike, then did his policy Let go the sluices, and triped up the heals Of the whole body of their troop, that stood Within the violent current of the stream. Their front beleaguered betwixt the water and The Town; seeing the flood was grown too deep, To promise them a safe retreat; exposed The force of all their spirits, (Like the last Expiring gasp of a strong hearted man) Upon the hazard of one charge; but were Oppressed and fell. The rest that could not swim, Were only drowned; but those that thought to escape By swimming, were by murderers that flankerd, The level of the flood both drow'nd and slain. Walking next day upon the fatal shore, Among the slaughtered bodies of their men, Which the full-stomack'd Sea had cast upon The sands, it was m'vnhappy chance to light Upon a face, whose favour when it lived My astonished mind informed me I had seen. He lay in's Armour; as if that had been His Coffin, and the weeping Sea (Like one; Whose milder temper does lament the death Of him whom in his rage he slew) runs up The Shore; embraces him; kisses his cheek, Goes back again and forces up the Sands To bury him; and every time it parts, Sheds tears upon him; till at last (as if It could no longer endure to see the man Whom it had slain, yet loath to leave him;) with A kind of unresolved unwilling pace, Winding her waves one in another, Like A man that folds his arms, or wrings his hands For grief; ebbed from the body and descends: As if it would sink down into the earth, And hide it self for shame of such a deed. Your fear hath told you that whereof my grief Was loath to be the messenger. His forward spirit pressed into the front; And being engaged within the enemy, When they retreated through the rising stream; I'the violent confusion of the throng Was overborn and perished in the flood. Plot summary

Plot — The Atheist's Tragedy, or The Honest Man's Revenge
Montferrers has forbidden his son Charlemont from going to war and refused him the money necessary to equip himself. Charlemont gets a subvention from his uncle, D’Amville, and this persuades Montferrers to change his mind. But D’Amville is an atheist who believes that he must make his happiness in this world rather than wait for reward or punishment in the next, and he has an ulterior motive for wanting his nephew out of the way. Castabella loves Charlemont, and has privately agreed to marry him when he returns. They entrust their secret only to the Puritan Languebeau Snuffe. D’Amville bribes Snuffe to betray them and persuade Belleforest, her father, to make her marry Rousard, his son. Castabella is pressured into the match, despite the protestations of Sebastian, Rousard’s younger brother. As soon as he is married, Rousard falls ill with symptoms which include impotence. On the wedding day, D’Amville’s accomplice Borachio brings false news that Charlemont has been killed in battle. Montferrers makes a new will in D’Amville’s favour. D’Amville gets Montferrers’s torchbearers drunk at the wedding celebrations, and tricks them into quarrelling with one another when lighting their master home; the torches go out in the fight, allowing Borachio to murder Montferrers under cover of darkness. Montferrers’s ghost appears to Charlemont, tells him of the murder, and enjoins him not to take revenge. Charlemont returns to France to find himself presumed dead and his fiancée unhappily married. He confronts D’Amville and has an altercation with Sebastian, but another appearance by the ghost prevents him from getting carried away. D’Amville fabricates an unpaid debt in order to have Charlemont imprisoned, but Sebastian, who has been slightly injured, asks for money to pay a surgeon and uses it to pay the supposed debt and get Charlemont released. D’Amville takes steps to secure the future of his estate. Reasoning that Rousard will beget no son and that Sebastian, a libertine, will never marry, he decides that he will have to produce a bastard to be his heir, and selects Castabella as the mother. He commissions Borachio to murder Charlemont in the churchyard, but Charlemont kills Borachio instead. Snuffe uses the same churchyard for an assignation, intending to scare off any unwanted attention by disguising himself as the ghost of Montferrers, but he is himself frightened by Charlemont and runs away leaving the disguise behind.
