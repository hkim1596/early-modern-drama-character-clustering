To Cambria, Madam, with letters from ̄ the king. I, but you needs not care: and so must I, A handsome man, be quickly trust up, And when a man's hanged, all the world cannot save him, I am overjoyed, I surfeit of sweet words: Kind Queen, had I a hundred lines, I would Spend ninety Nine of them for you, for that word. That one life is not too deer for my good Queen; this sword, this buckler, this head, this heart, these hands, arms, legs, tripes, bowels, and all the members else whatsoever, are at your dispose; use me, trust me, command me: if I fail in any thing, tie me to a dung cart, and make a Scavengers horse of me, and whip me, so long as I have any skin on my back. A strong Bound, a firm Obligation, good in law, good in law: if I keep not the condition, let my neck be the forfey ture of my negligence. And as bade a tongue if it be set on it, as any Oysterwife at Billingsgate hath: why, I have made many of my neighbours forsake their houses with railing upon them, and go dwell else where; and so by my means houses have been good cheap in our parish: My tongue being well whetted with choler, is more sharp then a Razor of Palerno. Commend me not, sweet Queen, before you try me. As my deserts are, so do think of me. I thirst to hear it. I fly, I fly. I have been ouer-negligent to day, In going to the Temple of my God, To render thanks for all his benefits, Which he miraculously hath bestowed on me, In raising me out of my mean estate, When as I was devoid of worldly friends, And placing me in such a sweet content, As far exceeds the reach of my deserts. My kingly husband, my rrour of his time, For zeal, for justice, kindness, and for care To God, his subjects, me, and Common weal, By his appointment was ordained for me. I cannot with the thing that I do want; I cannot want the thing but I may have, Save only this which I shall never obtain, My father's love, o this I never shall gain. I would abstain from any nutryment, And pine my body to the very bones: Bore foot I would on pilgrimage set forth Unto the furthest quarters of the earth, And all my life time would I sackcloth wear, And mourning-wise pour dust upon my head: So he but to forgive me once would please, That his grey hairs might go to heaven in peace. And yet I know not how I him offended, Or wherein justly I have deserved blame. O sisters! you are much to blame in this, It was not he, but you that did me wrong. Yet God forgive both him, and you and me, Even as I do in perfect charity. I will to Church, and pray unto my Saviour, That ere I die, I may obtain his favour. Now happily I am arrived here, Before the stately Palace of the Cambrian King: If Leir be here safe-seated, and in rest, To rouse him from it I will do my best. Now bags of gold, your virtue is (no doubt) To make me in my message bold and stout. The King of heaven preserve your Majesty. And send your Highness everlasting reign. Kind greetings from the Cornwall Queen: The residue these letters will declare. I did leave her at my parting, in good health. See how her colour comes and goes again, Now read as scarlet, now as pale as ash: She how she knits her brow, and bites her lips, And stamps, and makes a dumb show of disdain, Mixed with revenge, and violent extremes. Here will be more work and more crowns for me. She never yet committed trust to me, But that (I hope) she found me always faithful So will I be to any friend of hers, That hath occasion to employ my help. I have a heart compact of Adamant, Which never knew what melting pity meant. I weigh no more the murdering of a man, Then I respect the cracking of a Flay, When I do catch her biting on my skin. If you will have your husband or your father, Or both of them sent to another world, Do but command me doo't, it shall be done. I, this is it will make me do the deed: O, had I every day such customers, This were the gainefulst trade in Christendom! A purse of gold given for a paltry stab! Why, heres a wench that longs to have a stab. Well, I could give it her, and never hurt her neither. It is a world to see now I am flush, How many friends I purchase every where! How many seeks to creep into my favour, And kiss their hands, and bend their knees to me! No more, here comes the Queen, now shall I know her mind, And hope for to derive more crowns from her. I am a poor man, and it Like your Grace; But yet I always love to keep my word. I long to hear it, it might have been dispatched, If you had told me of it yesternight. It is more strange, that I am not by this Beside my self, with longing for to hear it. I would that word were in his belly again, It hath frighted me even to the very heart: This old man is some strong Magician: His words have turned my mind from this exploit. Then neither heaven, earth, nor hell be witness; But let this paper witness for them all. Shall I relent, or shall I prosecute? Shall I resolve, or were I best recant? I will not crack my credit with two Queens, To whom I have already passed my word. O, but my conscience for this act does tell, I get heavens hate, earth's scorn, and pains of hell. But I am not prepared for to strike. I, but you are unwise, to send and errand By him that never means to deliver it: Why, he must go along with you to heaven: It were not good you should go all alone. Marry, but it does, sir, by your leave; your good days are passed: though it be no matter for you, 'tis a matter for me, proper men are not so rife. Why that am I, let that never trouble thee. I am as hard to be moved as another, and yet me thinks the strength of their persuasions stirs me a little. I am as wilful as you for your life: I will not do it, now you do entreat me. Beshrew you for it, you have put it in me: The parlosest old men, that ere I herd. Well, to be flat, isle not meddle with you: Here I found you, and here isle leave you: If any ask you why the case so stands? Say that your tongue as were better then your hands. Unto your father, if he be there. Madam, I hope your Grace will stand Between me and my neck-verse, if I be Called in question, for opening the Kings letters. It suffices, conceit it is already done: I will so toung-whip him, that I will Leave him as bore of credit, as a Poulter Leaves a Cony, when she pulls off his skin. Few words are best in so small a matter: These are but trifles. By this book I will. Plot — The True Chronicle of King Leir (King Lear and his Three Daughters)
King Lear deems it a good moment to marry off his three daughters: with his wife recently dead, they no longer have a mother to direct them. His plan is to retire and divide the kingdom between them as their dowries, and to marry them to neighbouring kings in order to forestall foreign aggression. Cordella, however, is determined to marry for love rather than for diplomatic convenience, so Lear plans to circumvent her by laying on a test: the daughters will make a public declaration of how much they love their father, and he will then ask Cordella to show that love by complying with his wishes over her marriage. Scaliger warns the other daughters, Goneril and Regan, of what Lear has in mind, and misleads them into believing that their shares of the kingdom will reflect the extent of their declared love. Jealous of their younger sister’s beauty and renown, they flatter Lear so that Cordella’s simple statement of due filial love pales in comparison. Lear gives the kingdom wholly to them, summons the Kings of Cornwall and Cambria to be their husbands, and assigns them their shares by lot. Against the advice of the good counsellor Perillus, Lear banishes Cordella from court, and she plans to make her living working as a seamstress. By chance, she meets the King of France, who has come to Britain disguised in search of a wife, and he takes her back to France as his Queen. Lear goes to live with Cornwall and Goneril, but annoys her with his criticisms of her extravagance. Scaliger advises her that halving his allowance will persuade him to take himself off to Regan, and so indeed it does. Cornwall sends a messenger to Regan asking for news of Lear, but Goneril bribes him to deliver instead a libellous letter about her father’s behaviour; she also encourages him to murder Lear. Regan has the same idea, and sets up an opportunity, but at the last moment the messenger is struck by conscience, admits the truth to his intended victim: and runs away.
