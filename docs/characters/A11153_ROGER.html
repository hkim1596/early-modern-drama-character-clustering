Yes, mistress, he will come: but pray resolve Me one thing for my long service; What Business have you with the Churchman? Is it to make your Will, or to get you a new Husband? Then I would desire you to remember me, Mistress, I have served you long, and that's the best Service to a woman: make a good Will if you Mean to die, that it may not be said, Though Most women be long lived, yet they all die With and ill-will. Why then remember your self mistress; Take heed how you give away the head; It stands yet upon the shoulders of Your widowhood; the loving embracing Ivy Has yet the upper place in the house; If you give it to the Holly, take heed, There's pricks in Holly; or if you fear not The pricks, take heed of the wands, you Cannot have the pricks without the wands; You give away the sword, and must defend Your self with the scabbard; these are pretty Instructions of a friend; I would be loath to See you cast down, and not well taken up. I will first give him a caveat, to use you As kindly as he can. If you find my mistress Have a mind to this coupling at barleibreak, Let her not be the last couple to be left in hell. If she have a mind to a fresh husband, or So, use her as well as you can; let her enter Into as easy bans as may be. Oh mistress, where are you? I think you are the fortunatest Woman, that ever breathed of two shoes: the thief is Found. Bring him away Jug; nay, you shall see the strangest Piece of felony discovered that ever you saw, Or your great grandmothers Grandam before, or after, A pirate, a water thief. Bring him away Jug; yet the villain would not Confess a word till it was found about him. Did you not loose your wedding Ring the other day? No; well, thank him that brings it Home then; and will ask nothing for his pains. You see this Sammon? It cost but six pence: but had the Fisher known The worth of it, 'twould have cost you forty shillings. Is not this your Ring? Your maid Joan examining this Sammon, that she Bought in the Market, found that he had swallowed This Gudgeon. Alas, blame not the poor fish, mistress, he being a fleg, matique Creature, took Gold for Restorative. He took it fair, And he that gets Gold, let him eat Gold. Now do I see the old proverb come to pass; Give a woman luck, and cast her into sea: There's many a man would wish his wife good Luck, on that condition he might throw her Away so. But mistress, there's one within would Speak with you, that vexes as fast against Crosses, as you do against good luck. I'll tell her so for my own credits sake. I had rather help you to a close, stool, An't please you. There was some pleasure ere you came to that. That could be but little grief. But it may be he has not that should please her. We have hunted well, mistress; do you not see The hare's in sighed? Nay, I'l be sworn upon a book of Callico for that. Then you do wrong, Sir, for you take money for him: What woman can have a husband, but you must Have custom for him? and often the beware proves Nought too not worth the Impost. Nay, at the second hand you'll have a fee too; You fell in the Church, and they bring him again To your Churchyard, you must have tollage: Me thinks if a man die whither you will or no, He should be buried whither you would or no. Mistress make him your friend, for he knows what rate Good husbands are at; if there hath been a dearth Of women of late, you may chance pick Out a good prize; but take heed of a Clerk. Mistress, it's fasting day to day, there's nothing but fish. Marry there is Sammon, Pike, and fresh Cod, Soles, Maids, and Plaice. Nay mistress, I'll help him too; the maids shall first Dress the Pike, and the Cod, and then I'll dress The maids in the place you wot on. You have had two husbands to my knowledge; And if you had not one joyful hour between Both, I would you were hanged faith. That was a tedious time indeed. I, cursed widows are; but if they had all stiff husbands To tame'm, they be quiet enough. I care not if I do mistress, now my stomach's ready; Yet I'll stay a little and be but to vex you. I will not go yet. Nay then I'll go to vex you. Nay Sir, you do wrong to give a woman so many lies, She had rather have had twice so many standings, than One lie. Little less I promise you, if you lie with him. You may talk with me Sir, in the mean time. I do forbid the Banes: Alas poor Shagragge, My Mistress does but gull him; you may Imagine it to be TO welfe-day at night, and the Bean found in the corner of your Cake, but 'Tis not worth a fetch I'l assure you. You love to be merry Mistress; Come, come, we him four Farthings, and let him go. He'l pray for his good Dame, and be drunk; Why, if your blood does itch that way, we'll Stand together; how think you? Where he has mistaken the place a little, Being his wedding-day; he is in nomine, When he should be in re. In your Countinghouse; If he were a kind Husband, he would have been in another Countinghouse by this time: he's tumbling Over all his money bags yonder; you shall Hear of him in the bowling Alley again. Look where he comes. Now you may see what hasty matching is; You had thought to have been vexed, and now You cannot: You have married a husband, That, Sir reverence of the title, now being my Master in law, I do think he'll prove the miserablest, covetous Rascal, that ever beat beggar from his gate. But It's no matter; time was when you were fairly Offered, if you would have took it; you might have had Other matches y'faith, if it had pleased you; and those That would have crossed you; I would have sold away All that ever you had had; have kept two or three Whores at livery under your nose; have turned you out In your smock, and have used you Like a woman; where As now, if you'd hang your self, you can have none of These blessings: but it's well enough, now you must Take what follows. Marry mistress here are two creatures S e able to make one man, desires to speak With you. Nay, I know that already; the one is a Thing that was pluc'd into the World, by the head and shoulders to be Wondered at, and it's called a knight; the other Is a coach-horse of the same over-ridden raze; And that's a foolish Gentleman. And they be loath to keep their beds i'th'counter Mistress; they are afraid of Sergeants, Master Lambskin, Knows that Mace is a binder. I, you have been a lose liver always, It's time to look to you. Do you not smell Poultry beware, Sir Godfry? Here's my master now Gentlemen. You should be a Calf by your white face. I'll make them drink if they will; come Gallants empty your bags, and I'l bombast Your bellies; this lean Gentleman looks As if he had no lining in' guts, I could Take him by the leg and hurl him into The dog-house.

Plot summary

Plot — A New Wonder, A Woman Never Vexed
Old Forster has recently made his fortune by marrying a rich widow, but they are both dissatisfied with the financial irresponsibility of his brother and son, whom ===== PAGE 17 ===== 1624 they are on the point of disowning. Robert, the son, has purloined some of his stepmother’s money to buy his uncle, Stephen Forster, out of debtors’ prison; he also gives Stephen money to buy new clothes. Stephen uses the money for gambling, and is cheated. Robert is disappointed to find him in the gaming-house, and he himself likewise gets into trouble with his stepmother for going there, even though he only went in to look for Stephen. Mistress Forster has an uncommonly even- tempered friend, another rich widow, who enjoys miraculous good fortune. When she loses her wedding ring in the river, it turns up inside a salmon bought by her maid. She meets Stephen at the gaming-house and likes him. She suggests that he should marry for money, and offers herself. He accepts, and they perform a hand-fasting. Jane is being pursued by two undesirable suitors, an old knight and a young fool. Her father, Alderman Brune, suggests Robert Forster as a third option, if he can be reconciled with his father. Brune is Old Forster’s partner in a mercantile venture, but accepts Forster’s offer to buy him out; he takes payment in cloth rather than cash, which clears Forster’s warehouses for the new merchandise due in any day. However, Forster continues to look askance at Robert. News arrives that the ships have sunk in the Thames: Forster is bankrupt, and voluntarily commits himself to prison in order to avoid his creditors. Stephen takes charge of his new wife’s property, and proves, against expectations, to be provident rather than spendthrift. He asks Robert to help him manage the estate, and adopts him as his heir, insisting only that he does nothing to help his father. However, Stephen himself pays the prison-keeper to ensure that his brother remains comfortable. When Jane’s two other suitors complain of the favour shown to Robert, Stephen produces evidence that they are in debt to the widow’s first husband (and therefore now to him), and the threat of arrest gets rid of them. The widow generously accepts their payment of £200 to discharge a debt of ten times that amount. Unable to resist his sense of filial obligation, Robert purloins the money and gives it to his father. Stephen pretends to be angry, but secretly pays off his brother’s creditors and thereby secures his release.…
