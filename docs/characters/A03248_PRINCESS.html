A kind Husband I'll warrant him: if ever I chance to bride, Heaven grant I find no worse. As kind a Father as a Husband now: If ever I chance to wed, such Heaven grant me. You ever long She may both dream, and speak as much as I. No question but she thinks as much already; And were here voice and her election free, She would not stick to say this man for me. Why to change face, They say in mod st Maids are signs of grace: Yet many that Like her hold down the head, Will never change colour when they're once in bed. Not out of heart; for all of her complexion, Show in their face the fire of their affection: And even the modest wives, this know we too, Oft blush to speak what is no shame to do. Here enters one, I hope it be not he. My Lord, how Like you this? Shee'd blush to speak, that does not blush to kiss. Is this your sweetheart? I had needs wish you much joy, for I see but a little towards: Where did you take him up by the hye-way, or did you not fallen in love with him hanging on a Gibbet? And in faith Lady can you be in love with this rag of honour? But say your Father now, as many Father's are, prove a true worldling, and rather than bestow thee on one dejected, disinherit thee? how then? By my faith good counsel; if I live long enough, It may be I may have the grace to follow it. A fairer face in Court who ever saw? Her beaut, would become the name of Queen. For grace and feature England affords not a more complete Virgin. This noble Lady, now my royal Mother, Hath by her love to you, regard to us, And courteous affability to all, Attained the general suffrage of the Realm, Her modest carriage shall be rules to me, Her words instructions, her behaviour precepts, Which I shall ever study to observe. But your Queen, That virtuous Lady, when I think on her, I can but grieve at her dejectedness. Or for the quantity better contrived: This Lord in all his actions is still noble, Exceeding all requital. You have not my consent yet. Why then on thee: He that the Father does so much respect, Should not methinks the daughters love despise. It's good for Maids take Husbands when they may. Heaven knows how long we may be forced to stay. Here's one falls off from me. Ey so they say, But unto me it is a pach unknown; Yet that which cheers me, I shall do no more Than those, and such as I, have done before. Sure it's a thing that must, though without skill, Even when you please, I am ready for your will. Stay. O Father, hear me for my Husbands life, Doubly allied, I am his Niece and Wife. And Husband unto me. Your Mother and your Daughter. Speak, what hath he done?

Plot summary

Plot — The Royal King and the Loyal Subject
The King of England returns home from a crusade, where his life was saved in battle by the Marshal. He rewards the Marshal with eminence, which makes the other lords jealous. At a hunt, the King’s horse loses a shoe; the Marshal gives him the shoes off his own horse. He also magnanimously allows the Prince to beat him in a tournament. The envious lords try to persuade the King that the Marshal’s humility is a cloak for pride and ambition, and that he wishes to be greater than the King himself in his magnanimity. The King resolves to test the Marshal, and treats him badly at a public feast: he is dismissed from office and his job is handed over to his greatest enemy, Chester; then he is told to leave the court altogether. He retires to the country, but the King orders him to send his favourite daughter to court for some sexual purpose. The Marshal sends his elder daughter, Isabella, subject to the condition that the King should return her if he dislikes her; and gives her instructions that she should conceal any pregnancy and constantly tell the King that her sister is better looking. The King makes Isabella his Queen, and, unbidden, the Marshal sends a generous dowry. She becomes pregnant, but does as her father instructs; she also tells her husband of her sister Katherine’s superior beauty. Affronted, the King sends her home along with the dowry and orders the Marshal to send Katherine. The Marshal claims that Katherine is ill and unable to travel, but promises to send her when she is better. He does more than that: he sends back both daughters with the dowry doubled, continuing to outdo the King in magnanimity. The Prince is allowed to marry Katherine. The Marshal asks leave to return to court with a rich gift for the King, which turns out to be his new-born son by Isabella. The King tries to trump even this by giving the Marshal a new wife, the Princess. At the wedding, however, the Marshal seems discontented: he is worried that his fortunes depend on the pleasure of his new wife. The envious Chester encourages the King to take offence, and the Marshal is summoned from his bed, tried, and sentenced to death. He accepts his fate with dignity, but his and the King’s children all plead for his life. The King declares that the Marshal’s loyalty has been thoroughly tested and proven.
