The confines of his Government, whose view Is the pretext of his Command, hath need Of no such sudden expedition. 'Tis strange a man that had, through his life passed, So sure a foot in virtue and true knowledge, As Clermont D'Ambois, should be now found tripping, And taken up thus, so to make his fallen More steep and headlong. There was a merit for this, in the fault That Bussy made, for which he (doing penance) Proves that these foul adulterous guilts will run Through the whole blood, which not the clear can shun. 'Tis safe and common. The more your friend dares trust, the more deceive him. And as through dewy vapours the Suns form Makes the gay Rainbow, girdle to a storm, So in hearts hollow, Friendship (even the Sun To all good growing in society) Makes his so glorious and divine name hold Colours for all the ill that can be told. Do as you please; his blameless spirit deserves (I dare engage my life) of all this, nothing. Who knows not The bombast polity thrusts into his Giant, To make his wisdom seem of size as huge, And all for sleight encounter of a shade, So he be touched, he would have heinous made? What spirit breathes thus, in this more then man, Turns flesh to air possessed, and in a storm, Tears men about the field Like Autumn leaves? He turned wild lightning in the Lackeys hands, Who, though their sudden violent twitch unhorsed him, Yet when he boar himself, their saucy fingers Flew as too hight off, as he had been fire. The ambush then made in, through all whose force, He drove as if a fierce and fire given Canon Had spit his iron vomit out amongst them. The Battles then, in two halfmoons enclosed him, In which he showed, as if he were the light, And they but earth, who wondering what he was; Shrunk their steel horns, and gave him glorious pass, And as a great shot from a town besieged, At foes before it, flies forth black and roaring, But they too far, and that with weight oppressed, (As if disdaining earth) does only grass, Strike earth, and up again into the air; Again Sinks to it, and again does rise, And keeps such strength that when it softliest moves, It piecemeal shivers any let it proves: So flew brave Clermont forth, till breath forsook him, Then fell to earth, and yet (sweet man) even then His spirits convulsions made him bound again, Passed all their reaches; till all motion spent, His fixed eyes cast a blaze of such disdain, All stood and stared, and untouched let him lie, As something sacred fallen out of the sky. Oh now some rude hand hath laid hold on him! See, prisoner led, with his bans honoured more, Then all the freedom he enjoyed before. I grieve that virtue lives so undistinguished From vice in any ill, and though the crown Of Sovereign Law; she should be yet her footstool, Subject to censure, all the shame and pain Of all her rigor. Plot — The Revenge of Bussy D'Ambois
Before the start of the action, the Duke of Guise had Bussy D’Amboise and his brother Clermont brought ===== PAGE 63 ===== 1610 to court. Montsoreau had Bussy killed in reprisal for his adultery with Tamyra, and he died prophesying the deaths of Monsieur and Guise; his ghost then appeared to Clermont demanding revenge. Their sister Charlotte also seeks to avenge Bussy, and made her marriage to Baligny conditional on his swearing to participate in the plot; but Clermont insists on doing it alone, as the ghost commanded. Baligny’s role is, therefore, to deliver Clermont’s challenge to Montsoreau; but he cannot get access because Montsoreau has fearfully barricaded himself into his house. Two allies help him. Renel, who is selling his lands to Montsoreau, enables him to get in. When Montsoreau refuses to accept the challenge, Baligny leaves it behind anyway and Tamyra, who has reluctantly gone back to her husband, promises to make him read it. Unknown to the others, Baligny is reporting to the King on potential troublemakers like Clermont and Renel. The King is concerned at the growing power of Guise’s faction, so he and Baligny arrange for Clermont to be arrested: Clermont is to travel with Baligny to inspect the troops at Cambrai, and there he will be taken into custody by Baligny’s lieutenant, Maillard; thus Baligny will not have to participate directly in his brother-in-law’s downfall. Clermont has to leave Paris before concluding his business with Montsoreau. In Cambrai, he receives an anonymous letter warning him of Baligny’s treachery, but refuses to credit it. Maillard arrests him, with difficulty, and he is taken back to the Bastille; but he is allowed to send a message to his lover, the Countess of Cambrai, who was expecting him to visit her that evening. ~ Guise pressurizes the King into signing an order for .
