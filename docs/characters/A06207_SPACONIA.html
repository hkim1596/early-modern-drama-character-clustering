Nay, let me stay alone, And when you see Armenia again, You shall behold a Tomb more worth then I, Some friend that either loves me, or my cause, Will built me something to distinguish me From other women: Many a weeping verse He will lay on, and much lament those maids That place their loves unfortunately hight, As I have done, where they can never reach: But why should you go to Iberia? That conquered thee, Tigranes he has wone but half of Thy body; but thy mind may be as free As his, his will did never combat thine, And take it prisoner: Oh Tigranes, I know you are to see a Lady there, To see, and Like I fear: perhaps the hope Of her makes you forget me ere we partly, Be happier then you know to wish: farewell. Can you imagine that a longing maid When she beholds you, can be pulled away With words from loving you? Why, I had rather lose you: Can my heart Consent to let my tongue throw out such words, And I that ever yet spoke what I thought, Shall find it such a thing at first to lie: Sir I shall find no want of that. I will not take upon me to govern your tongue Sir, you shall call me what you please: Thalestris. My truth. Above my knee? I hope your Grace knows him so well already, I shall not need to tell you he's vain and foolish: 'Tis true, he is not, would he would seem less: I know a Lady may, and more I fear Another Lady will. I do beseech you Madam send away Your other women, and receive from me A few sad words, which set against your joys, May make um shine the more. I kneel a stranger here to beg a thing Unfit for me to ask, and you to grant, 'Tis such another strange ill laid request, As if a beggar should entreat a King To leave his Sceptre and his Throne to him, And take his rags to wander over the World Hungry and cold. As ill is mine. Alas, 'tis of that nature, that it must Be uttered, I, and granted, or I die: I am ashamed to speak it; but where life Lies at the stake, I cannot think her woman, That will not take something unreasonably To hazard saving of it: I shall seem A strange petitioner, that wish all ill To them I beg of, ere they give me ought, Yet so I must: I would you were not fair, Nor wise, for in your ill consists my good: If you were foolish, you would hear my prayer; If foul, you had not power to hinder me: He would not love you. Nay, my request is more without the bounds Of reason yet; for 'tis not in the power Of you to do what I would have you grant. Your brother brings a Prince into this Land Of such a noble shape, so sweet a grace, So full of worth withal, that every maid That looks upon him, gives away her self To him for ever; and for you to have He brings him: and so mad is my demand, That I desire you not to have this man, This excellent man, for whom you needs must die, If you should miss him. I do now expect You should laugh at me. 'Tis by me, His own desire too, that you would not love him. 'Tis yet His own desire, but when he sees your face, I fear it will not be; therefore I charge you As you have pity, stop those tender ears From his enchanting voice, close up those eyes, That you may neither catch a dart from him, Nor he from you: I charge you as you hope To live in quiet, for when I am dead For certain I shall walk to visit him, If he break promise with me: for as fast As oaths without a formal ceremony Can make me, I am to him: Indeed it is not Spaconia is name; but I desire Not to be known to others: Oh, I fear Tigranes will be caught, he looks me thinks As he would change his eyes with her; some help There is above for me I hope. Plot summary

Plot — A King and No King
Arbaces has been away from his kingdom for many years fighting a war with Armenia, which he has just won. He has received reports from his Lord Protector, Gobrias, about the virtue and accomplishments of his younger sister Panthea, whom he has not seen since she was nine years old. He offers her hand in marriage to Tigranes, the vanquished Armenian King, but Tigranes demurs. Arbaces takes him back to Iberia to see her for himself. Tigranes’ objection lies in the fact that he already has a girlfriend, Spaconia, and he bribes the cowardly Bessus to bring her along and place her in Panthea’s household. Once there, she begs Panthea not to marry Tigranes, and Panthea agrees. Arane, Arbaces’ mother, has been trying to assassinate him since he came to the throne. Another attempt is foiled, but he pardons her when he returns in triumph. She clearly has an ulterior motive which is known to Gobrias, but both are discreet about its precise nature. Arbaces’ first meeting with Panthea as an adult proves unsettling: he falls in love with her. So does Tigranes. Identifying a rival, Arbaces imprisons him. Torn between desire and conscience, he also imprisons Panthea. Panthea gives Spaconia a token authorizing her to visit Tigranes in prison. He rejects her, but Arbaces finds them together and assumes that she is a go-between. Gobrias importunes him to release Panthea, who persuades him that his suspicions are unfounded: she loves him, and would act upon that love if to do so were not incestuous. The Armenians send Spaconia’s father as an ambassador to negotiate for Tigranes’ release. He believes his daughter has eloped with Bessus, but his denunciations of her bring Tigranes to his senses: he will make Spaconia his Queen. When Arbaces decides to kill Gobrias for corrupting him to incest, the truth comes out. Arbaces is not of royal blood. The old King married Arane when he was old and impotent, and, fearful about the succession, Gobrias and Arane conspired to produce an heir: Gobrias gave Arane his new-born son, and she faked his birth. Later, it proved that the King was not so impotent after all, and he died leaving Arane pregnant with Panthea, but by the time this was known, the young Arbaces had already succeeded to the throne. Since then, Gobrias and Arane have both been trying to put matters to rights, Arane with her murder plots and Gobrias by encouraging Arbaces to marry his putative sister and so establish the legitimacy of his teign.
