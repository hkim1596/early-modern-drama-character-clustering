The accidents which in our nonage chance, A ripened age not to remembrance brings, Like fabulous dreams which darkness does advance, That are by day disdained as frivolous things: For, our conceptions are not then so strong That they can leave impressions long behind, Yet mix (deer friend) old griefs new joys among, And call afflicted infancy to mind. Though now my breast does greater thoughts embrace, Of youthful sport, yet do not spare to speak; "Let cares alternately give pleasure place: "That which is bent still, must sometime break. Some of the wisemen then I herd remained, Who from their former sentence did recoil, And said no danger was, since I had reigned, Then did dismiss me for my native soil; Where when I had my blooming season spent, To weakened wrath your lines did strength afford, Informing us that many Medes were bent For his great cruelty to leave their Lord; And wished (if to their Sceptre I aspired) That I should move the Persians to rebel, Which did succeed even as my soul desired: For they disdained in bondage base to dwell; When my encouraged troops all armed did stand, Ere they from Strangers could attend relief, I quickly marched, encountering with that band, Of which the King had chanced to make you chief. And have you then not herd, his death but proved The black beginning of a bloody Scene? His wife Panthea at the first not moved, Seemed as she had some marble image been; The body that had oft her fancies fired, She caused bear out of sighed, still deer, though dead; But where the River ran, when once retired, She betwixt her bosoms Rounds entombed his head; And then from Rage she borrowed some relief: For sorrow by degrees, a passage seeks, Vapouring forth sighs, which made a cloud of grief, A mighty storm of tears rained down her cheeks; Then, whilst her Eyes the wonted object missed, With heavy looks resolving fatal haste, Pale senseless lips she prodigally kissed, With as great ardour then as in times passed. I posted thither, bent to have relieved This Lady of a portion of her woes. Heaven bear me witness! I was greatly grieved, Who would to save one friend, spare hosts of foes, She first a space me passionately eyed, Then with those words, her lips did slowly move, My husband, lo, hath valorously died, As of your friendship, worthy of my love. "My coming but increased griefs starving store: "For, till that passion of it self expire, "All kind of comfort but augments it more, "Like drops of oil thrown on a mighty fire. A constant countenance though I strived to make, And what her woes diminish might, did tell; That comfort which I gave, I could not take, And scarcely could throw forth my last farewell; When I had left her but a little space, She did discharge the Eunuchs from her sighed, Then prayed her Nurse to bury in one place Her and her Lord, as they deserved of right; Last, looking on his corpse, she drew a sword, And even as if her soul had flown in him, (Pure snows in Crimson died) embraced her Lord, Whilst beauty's blubbered Stars were waxing dim; Then bent to fallen, when her they could not raise, (As scorning to survive their prosperous state) In emulation of their Ladies praise, The Eunuchs did precipitate their fate. Oh sweet Panthea, rich in rarest parts, I must admire thy ghost though thou be gone! Who mightest have made a Monarchy of hearts, Yet loathed unlawful loves, and loved but one; Oh wondrous wonders, wonders wondrous rare! A woman constant, such a beauty chased, A mind so pure, joined with a face so fair, With virtue beauty in one person placed; Both were well matched as any could devise, Whose death confirms the union of their life; He valorous, she virtuous, both wise, She worthy such a Mate, he such a wife. And Harpagus, lest that it should be thought, That of brave minds the memory may die, Cause built a stately Tomb with Statues wrought, Where both their bodies with respect may lie. "On those whom they have wronged, none should rely: "Just rancour unrevenged, can never die. Behold how Craesus with his riches blind, Durst even encounter with my warlike band; And whilst a prosperous course betrayed his mind, Did not suspect what power was in my hand; But he and his confederates have seen How victory does still my troops attend, And Persia must be once all Asia's Queen, On whom for servants Princes shall depend; Now Croesus is overcome, this Town surprised, And Lydia charged with gold, does yield rich spoils; The League unprosperous, Egypt hath despised, This is the happy end of all our toils. But ah! one sour unseasons all my sweets, Whose praise through all the peopled circuit fleets, And with his love each generous courage warms; Then when (though weak in troops) in courage strong, the Egyptian Chariots desperately he charged There (whilst he fought infortunately long) Mars from terrestrial bans his soul enlarged. As for old Croesus, I am else resolved, He with some captives whom I keep in store Shall have their bodies by the fire dissolved, As offerings to the Gods whom I adore. My Soldiers pains this City shall defray, Since by their means it hath been gained for us, I yield it unto them, as their just prey, Who taste the sweetness of their travels thus; Of other things we shall so well dispose That our renown through all the world shall shine, Till Cyrus name give terror to all those, Who dare against his Sovereignty repine.

Plot summary

Plot — Croesus
Solon visits Croesus, hoping to persuade him to give up his immoderate love of gold and think about less transitory things. He explains that all mortality is subject to change, and tells Croesus stories about the importance of posthumous fame, but Croesus considers them irrelevant to his situation as a king. Solon realizes that Croesus wili change only by losing the power which has made him arrogant. Adrastus is banished from Phrygia after accidentally killing his brother. (His intended victim was the man who poisoned his girlfriend.) Croesus has a nightmare about the death of his son Atys, and becomes excessively protective: he makes Atys marry in order to secure the succession, appoints him a bodyguard, and bans the type of sharp implement which caused his death in the dream. Mysia is ravaged by a wild boar, and its people appeal to Croesus for help. He is happy to hear that they don’t want his money, but distressed that they ask instead that he send Atys to hunt the boar. Atys protests at his father’s reluctance, so Croesus accedes, assigning Adrastus to look after him during the mission. Atys is killed, and Adrastus kills himself in remorse. In the past, Astyages, King of Media and Croesus’ brother-in-law, was perturbed by a prophecy that he would be deposed by his grandson. When the child was born, he tried to have it killed; but the herdsman who was given the job spared its life and adopted it as a replacement for his own stillborn baby. During his youth, Cyrus’ princely behaviour led to his identification. Astyages served Harpagus a banquet of his own son’s flesh, so Harpagus contacted Cyrus and raised a rebellion. Now Cyrus has led the Persian army to depose Astyages. Against his counsellor’s advice, Croesus plans to intervene and conquer Persia, but Cyrus anticipates him and attacks pre-emptively, ordering that Croesus be taken alive. Croesus is nearly killed by a Persian soldier, but saved when his dumb son cries out and identifies him. Cyrus intends to burn him at the stake, but is impressed by Croesus’ acknowledgement that Solon was right to warn him not to trust in worldly things. Cyrus frees him, and orders restraint in his soldiers’ looting.
