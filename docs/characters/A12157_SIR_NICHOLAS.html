WHere's Marque-text my Chaplain. Bid him read Prayers in the Diningroom. I won't pray too day, dost here? Bid my Tutor come down to me. Why he that reads Travail to me, the wit that I took up in Pauls in a tiffany Cloak without a hatband, now I have put him into a doublet of Sattaine — stay he's here. Enter Tutor. Morrow Tutor, what hour take you it? How? Very learnedly then I was ready between Six and sevens too day. Yes, yes Sir. In troth Sir, I remember some such things but I have forgotten they. Why and Istmus, is and Elbow of land. A neck? Why I was near it, if you had let me alone, I should have come up too't. And Island is and high Mountain, which shoots it self into the Sea. Is't so? And Island then is, no matter let it go, it's not the first Island we would lost. As perfect in them as I am in these, faith I shall never con these things handsomely, may not a man study Travail without these Circles, Degrees and altilatitudes you speak of? I do not care for the nearest way, I would time e nough to go about. I, and if please you I will have it stand in my hall to make my Tenants wonder instead of the book of Martyrs. What say you to England. I am resolved to be ignorant of my own country say no more on't, what think you if I went over to France the first thing I did. Dee think I would no more wit then to venture my self i'th salt water, I had rather be pickled and powdered at home by half that I had. No? Cuts? We have had too many French cuts already. A bald reason. He had no found head that invented it. Upon Taffety. Open as much as you will but no valour. That's when a man is drunk, is it not? But it is feign to be supported sometime with a bottom. Enter Servant. O my Father in law that shall be. Stay which is my wife? Are there not two Sir Nicholasses, pray what d'ee call this Gentlewoman? This is fine i'faith, well may a woman mistake her husband, when a man that is the wiser Vessel cannot know his own wife. A good jest i'faith, hark you, were you ever Catechized? Why, I know she meant to be my wife, and only I would married her, as folks go to Law, by Attourney, she is but her Deputy, for the more state I married her by proxy. Sayest thou so? Come hither Madam Treedle, Gentlemen, you think now, I have but and ill match on't, and that as they say I am cheated, do not believe it — a Lady is a Lady, a bargain is a bargain, and I'm a Knight & no Gentleman, so much for that — I grant I married her, in her Mistress's name, and though (as great men, that use to choose wives for their favourites or Servants, when they have done with they) I could put her off to my Footman or my Tutor here, I won't, I will maintain her my wife and publish her, d'ee see, publish her to any man that shall laugh at it, my own Ladybird, and Madam Treedle. Tutor we pardon. That's dangerous. When you please, name your own time I'm ready to be married at midnight. Plot summary

Plot — The Witty Fair One
PLOT After a dinner party at his brother Worthy’s house, Sir George Richly goes off to visit his prospective son-in-law Sir Nicholas in the country, leaving his daughter Violetta in Worthy’s care and under the watchful eye of his servant Brains: he is concerned that, as his heir, she should not be the object of unwelcome attentions from gallants. The other dinner guests take a walk in the garden. Aimwell has fallen for Violetta and asks Worthy what his chances are; he gets a frank answer, and resolves to give up his suit, but changes his mind when Violetta’s own words seem to imply that her father’s matrimonial plans for her may not have her full consent. Meanwhile Fowler woos Worthy’s daughter Penelope, but admits in private that he is not in earnest, only practising his skill. Sir George returns home with Sir Nicholas. He proves to be an arrant fool dependent on his opportunistic tutor, who can see that the objective of the marriage is one knight’s desire to control the other’s estate. Aimwell sends a letter to Violetta enclosed in one from Fowler to Penelope. Through a mix-up, Violetta keeps her reply and sends him back his original letter, which he takes as a rebuff. Brains steals the remaining letter from her and sends it to her father, who is outraged and plans to advance the date of the wedding. Violetta’s chambermaid Sensible, sacked by Sir George, tells Aimwell about the mistake with the letters; reassured that she loves him after all, he starts to lay plans. Importuned by Sir Nicholas’s tutor, Violetta agrees to elope with him rather than marry Sir Nicholas, on condition that he kills Brains. She gets permission to go out shopping, escorted by Brains, and the tutor picks a quarrel. While Brains’s attention is distracted, Violetta substitutes Sensible for herself. Brains discovers the ruse, but is arrested at the tutor’s suit before he can do anything about it. Brains writes asking Sir George to arrange for his release, and mentions the attempted elopement but not the substitution; so when Sir Nicholas confronts the tutor, it is Sensible, not Violetta, whom he takes back and marries. Meanwhile, Violetta and Aimwell are also married. Sir Nicholas and Sir George accept the fait accompli and forgive their servants. In the sub-plot, Fowler feigns a fever as a wooing technique; Penelope promises to marry him when he is better.
