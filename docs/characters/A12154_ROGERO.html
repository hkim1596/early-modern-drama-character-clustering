My Lord. Smell? not any thing my Lord to offend my nostril. Faith my Lord, they say that you are— A noble gentleman, a just and discreet Lord, and one that deserved to have his honours without money. I whisper? Not I good faith my Lord. Your Lordship has a pair on. How sir? Heaven defend. Good my Lord, you brought to the bar? Well sir. I accuse your good honour? for what I beseech you? I must be acquainted with some particulars first. Good my Lord it will not become me, being your hum ble servant. Why then have at you, first Signior Depazzi, thou art indicted of high Treason, hold up thy hand, guilty, or not guilty? Nay, very bade sir, answer I say, guilty or not guilty? 'Tis your best course to say so, well imagine Irise up the Duke's most learned in the Laws, and his nimble tongued Orator, have at your Signior. I will prove thou liest in thy throat, if thou deniest thy Treason, and so I address my self to the most understanding seats of Justice most wise, most honourable, and most in corrupt Judges, sleep not I beseech you, my place ath called me to plead in the behalf of my Prince and Country against this notable, this pernicious, and impudent Traitor, who hath plotted and contrived such high, heinous, and horrible treasons as no Age nor History hath ever mentioned the Like. Here he stands, whose birth I will not touch, because its altogether un known who begotten him: He was brought up among the small wares in the City, became rich by sinister and indirect practi ses, married a Merchant's wife at adventures, and was soon after advanced to be a Head-officer. Peace sirrah, peace, nay your Lordship's shall find him very audacious: This fellow not content to have his branches spread within the City, I speak it to his face, let him deny it, was afterwards by the corruption of his confederate, and the mere grace of his highness raised to honour, received infi nite favours from his Prince of blessed memory, yet Like a wretch, a villain, a viper, a Rat of Nilus, he hath practised Treasons against the sacred person of the Duke, for which he de serves not only to die, but also to suffer tortures, whips, racks, strappadoes, wheels, and all the fiery brazen bulls that can be in vented, as I shall make it appear to this honourable and ill lustrious Court. With all my heart I obey your Lordship's—thus then I pass from these circumstances, and proceed to the principal villainies that we have to lay to his charge. Imprimis thou Signior Depazzi didst offer to a groom a 100 crowns to poison his highness hunting saddle. Do not interrupt me varlet I will prove it, his hunting saddle, and woe shall be unto thy breech therefore, and finding this serpentive treason broken in the shell, do but lend your reverend ears to his next designs I will cut em off presently. This irreligious nay Atheistical Traitor, did with his own hands poison the Dukes prayer book, o impiety! and had his highness as in former times, he accustomed but prayed once in a month, which by special grace he omitted, how fatal had it been to Florence? but as by justice his excellence did then, and by his own want of devotion, prevent this assassinates purpose, so we hope in his own discretion, and the council of his state, he will take heed how he prays hereafter while he lives, to which every true subject will say Amen. Thou impudent brazen faced Traitor, wilt thou deny it? more over, and Like your good Lordship's, he hath for this fort night or three weeks before his apprehension, walked up and down the Court with a case of pistols charged wherewith, as he partly confessed, he intended to send the Duke to heaven with a powder. These are but sprinklings of his treason. Hold your self contented my Lord, he that is brought to the bar in case of treason, must look to have more objected then he can answer, or any man is able to justify. Mark, he will confess. That that my Lord hath overthrown him, he sayeth he never sought the princes life, ergo he sought his death, beside he hath herd of treason, now he that hears and discovereth not is equally guilty in fact: for in offences of this nature there are not accessaries, ergo he is a principal, and being a principal Traitor, he deserveth condemnation. No, traitors must not be suffered to speak, for when they have leave, they have liberty, and he that is a Traitor deserveth to be close Prisoner. I will speak truth, and I will be herd, and no man else in this place. You fit hear to do justice, I speak for the Duke and the safety of the common wealth. I defy all the world that will hear a Traitor speak, for him self, 'tis against the Law which provids that no man shall defend treason, and he that speaks for himself being a Traitor, does de fend his treason, thou art a Capital obstreperous malefactor. Plot summary

Plot — The Traitor
PLOT Lorenzo plots against both the Duke and his political rivals. He induces Pisano to jilt his fiancée Amidea and transfer his affections to the wealthy Oriana, who loves Cosmo; this will block Cosmo’s political ascent by denying him Oriana’s money. Cosmo, motivated by male friendship, agrees to give up his girlfriend. The Duke is warned against Lorenzo, but when he publicly confronts Lorenzo with the letter of denunciation, the traitor argues plausibly that it is a malicious libel. The sexually undisciplined Duke is attracted to Amidea, and Lorenzo arranges an assignation for him, but also inflames her brother Sciarrha with a desire for revenge: the liaison is to be the occasion for the Duke’s assassination. When he comes to Sciarrha’s, the Duke is shown a moral masque rebuking royal lust, but he is so busy chatting up Amidea that he doesn’t watch the entertainment. He visits Amidea in her bedroom, but she keeps him off by threatening suicide, and he returns to his senses. Sciarrha is satisfied: he denounces Lorenzo to the Duke, but when Lorenzo is told that the assassination has taken place, with the Duke eavesdropping, the traitor disclaims all knowledge and again re-establishes his good faith. De Pazzi, Lorenzo’s ally, worries that his part in the treason will lead to his execution, and asks to be excused from further complicity; he gets his wish to leave court when the Duke banishes him. Oriana’s mother, believing Cosmo is a poor match while his enemy Lorenzo is in the ascendant, forces her to marry Pisano; but Sciarrha kills the bridegroom on the way to the ceremony, to avenge his treatment of Amidea. Having persuaded the Duke to make another attempt on Amidea’s chastity, Lorenzo tries to enlist Sciarrha’s help by promising a pardon for Pisano’s murder. Sciarrha makes the proposition, but when Amidea consents to save his life, he saves her honour by killing her. Meanwhile Lorenzo recruits a new accomplice in Petruccio, and practises stabbing the Duke’s portrait. The Duke’s assignation with Amidea goes ahead, but he finds himself in bed with her corpse. Lorenzo and Petruccio assassinate him, but Lorenzo is killed by Sciarrha, who dies himself in the fight. Cosmo, the next in blood, becomes Duke.
