This is it I beg, And on my knees. The people of your Land, The Lycians, are through all the Nations That know their name, noted to have in use A vain and fruitless Superstition; So much more hateful, that it bears the show Of true Religion, and is nothing else But a self-pleasing bold lasciviousness. Many Ages before this, When every man God to himself a Trade, And was laborious in that chosen course, Hating and idle life, far worse then death: Some one that gave himself to wine and sloth, Which breed lascivious thoughts; And found himself conjoined For that by every painful man, To take his stain away, framed to himself A God, whom he pretended to obey, In being thus dishonest, for a name He called him Cupid. This created God, Men nature being ever credulous Of any vice that takes partly with his blood, Had ready followers enough: and since In every age they grew, especially Amongst your Subjects, who do yet remain Adorers of that drowsy Deity: Which drink invented: and the winged Boy, (For so they call him) has his sacrifices. These lose naked statutes through the Land, And in every Village, nay the palace Is not free from'em. This is my request, That these erected obscene Images May be plucked down and burned: and every man That offers to'em any sacrifice, may loose his life. Cleophila, what was he that went hence? I mean that handsome man, That something more then man I met at door. Come, he's some one You would preserve in private, but you want Cunning to do it, and my eyes are sharper Then yours, and can with one neglecting glance, See all the graces of a man. Who was't? That went hence now: I he. I think it he: how bravely he passed by: Is he not grown a goodly Gentleman? Oh blasphemy! he may perhaps to thee Appear deformed, for he is indeed Unlike a man: his shape and colours are Beyond the Art of Painting; he is Like Nothing that we have seen, yet does resemble Apollo, as I oft have fancied him, When rising from his bed he stirs himself, And shakes day from his hair. Cleophila, go send a page for him, And thou shalt see thy error, and repent. Alas what do I feel, my blood rebels, And I am one of those I used to scorn, My maiden-thoughts are fled against my self, I harbour Traitors in my Virginity, That from my childhood kept me company, Is heavier then I can endure to bear: Forgive me Cupid, for thou art a God, And I a wretched creature; I have sinned, But be thou merciful, and grant that yet I may enjoy what thou wilt have me, Love. Without deceit, whose heart does guide her tongue, Drowned in my passion, yet I will take leave. To call it reason that I door on thee. What answer wilt thou give me? If it be jest, not to esteem my life, Compared with thee: If it be jest in me, To hang a thousand kisses in and hour Upon those lips, and take they off again: If it be jest for me to marry thee, And take obedience on me while I live: Then all I say is jest: For every partly of this, I swear by those That see my thoughts, I am resolved to do. And I beseech thee, by thine own white hand, (Which pardon me, that I am bold to kiss With so unworthy lips) that thou wilt swear To marry me, as I do here to thee, Before the face of heaven. Plot summary

Plot — Cupid's Revenge
Leontius, King of Lycia, promises his daughter Hydaspes anything she asks for on her birthday. She wants him to ban the cult of Cupid in Lycia: the god’s naked statues are to be taken down and burnt, and Cupid-worship to be made a capital offence. Leontius agrees, persuaded that the religion is the cause of national immorality. His nephew Ismenus demurs: he will promulgate the Duke’s orders, but will himself take no hand in obeying them. As his temples are dismantled, Cupid declares vengeance on the kingdom. The Lycian royal family begins to behave with inappropriate amorousness. Hydaspes fixates on Zoilus, a deformed dwarf, and asks to be allowed to marry him; her father’s response is to have him beheaded, and she pines to death. Prince Leucippus falls for Bacha, a strumpet only interested in his wealth and power, but, questioned by his father, denies sexual misbehaviour and praises her virtue and chastity. Leontius is so impressed that he sends Leucippus away ===== PAGE 378 ===== and woos Bacha for himself, affecting youthful clothing and behaviour. Bacha becomes Queen of Lycia. When Leucippus returns to court, Bacha tries to seduce him again. He refuses to cuckold his father, and, annoyed at his refusal, she plots his death; this will leave her daughter Urania as the only heir to the throne. She makes Leontius jealous of his son’s popularity with the people and courtiers, and persuades him that Leucippus is plotting his assassination. The court parasite Timantus tells Leucippus that the King is to discuss his arrest at a council, and arranges to place him where he can eavesdrop and be forewarned of the charges. It is a ruse: Leucippus is arrested and sentenced to death, and Urania is proclaimed heir to the throne. At the execution, the citizens rise in rebellion and rescue the Prince from the scaffold. As Leontius sickens, Bacha schemes to marry Urania to a foreign prince; but she is in love with Leucippus, locates him through the good offices of Ismenus, and follows him posing as a page. Bacha engages Timantus to murder Leucippus. He finds the Prince by intercepting a letter from Ismenus; but his attack fails when Urania interposes her own body and is mortally wounded. Timantus is killed, but reveals her true identity before he dies. Ismenus brings Leucippus news that Leontius is dead. He declares that he will leave Bacha to heaven’s justice, but she stabs him.
