I, whispering, pray God it tend my good, Strange fear assails my heart, usurps my blood. Oh God, what means my father? Treason to my hearts truest sovereign, How soon is love smothered in foggy gain? Oh me accursed! I'll first be buried quick. I'll sooner be a sinner in forsaking Mother and father. You will subscribe to it, good, good, 'tis well, Love hath two chairs of state, heaven and hell: My deer Mounchensey, thou my death shalt rue, Ere to thy heart Milliscent prove untrue. I am not sweet, Passion is strong, when woe with woe does meet. While you desire his grief, my heart shall bleed. And I must take a twelve months approbation, That in mean time this sole and private life, At the years end may fashion me a wife: But sweet Mounchensey ere this year be done, Thou'st be a friar if that I be a Nun; And father ere young Ierninghams I'll be, I will turn mad to spite both him and thee. Alas sweet Love what shall become of me? I must to Chesson to the Nunry, I shall never see thee more. Dear sweet. No joy enjoys my heart till we next meet. Good men and true, stand together and hear your charge. This goes hard berladye. Worse and worse by Saint Marry. The holy maid will be no Nun. Madam it is the order of this place, When any virgin comes for approbation, Lest that for fear or such sinister practice, She should be forced to undergo this veil, Which should proceed from conscience and devotion: A visitor is sent from Uvaltham house, To take the true confession of the maid. What means the Friar? My heart misgives me, I should know that voice, You, who are you? The holy virgin bless me, Tell me your name, you shall ere you confess me. My Raimond, my deer heart, Sweet life give leave to my distracted soul, To wake a little from this swoon of joy, By what means camest thou to assume this shape? You are all sweet traitors to my poor old father, Oh my deer life, I was a dreamt to night, That as I was a praying in mine Psalter, There came a spirit unto me as I kneeled, And by his strong persuasions tempted me To leave this Nunry; and me thought, He came in the most glorious Angel shape, That mortal eye did ever look upon: Would, thou art sure that spirit, for theres no form, Is in mine eye so glorious as thine own. Well visitor, lest that perhaps my mother Should think the Friar too st ickt in his decrees, I this confess to my sweet ghostly father, If chased pure love be sin I must confess, I have offended three years now with thee. I'faith I cannot. Sweet life farewell; 'tis done, let that suffice, What my tongue fails I send thee by mine eyes. Sir, very well, I would to God we were at Brians lodge. Brother and friend, alas what shall we do? Brother Frank Jerningham, brother Clare. I, it is I sir. My brother Sir, and M. Jerningham, who hearing folks about us in the Chase, feared it had been sir Arthur and my father, who had pursued us, thus dispersed our selves till they were passed us. They be not far off, here about the grove. I brother, here. Alas, what shall we do? What noise was that? 'tis the watch, sure that villam nous unlucky rogue Smug is taine upon my life, and then all our villainy comes out, I herd one cry sure. With pardon sir, at name is quite undone, This truelove knot cancelles both maid and Nun. When first you told me I should act that partly, How cold and bloody it crept over my heart! To Chesson with a smiling brow I went, But yet, deer sir, it was to this intent, That my sweet Raimond might find better means, To steal me thence: in brief disguised he came, Like Novice to old father Hildersham. His tutor here did act that cunning partly, And in our love hath joined much wit to art. With pardon therefore we entreat your smiles, Love thwarted turns itself to thousand wiles. 'Tis well; Wheres Raimond brother? wheres my deer Mounchensey? Would we might weep together and then partly, Our sighing parley would much ease my heart. Alas sir, think you I shall ere be his? Oh Lord, hold your hands, I mean no harm sir. I am a maid sir, who? M. Brian? By the Lord there are some watchmen; I hear them name Master Constable, I would to God my Mill were and Eunuch and wanted her stones, so I were hence. 'Tis the Constable by this light, I'll steal hence, and if I can meet mine host Blague, isle tell him how Smug is taine, and will him to look to him self.

Plot summary

Plot — The Merry Devil of Edmonton
The magician Peter Fabell’s bargain with the devil falls due, and Coreb comes to collect his soul; but Fabell traps him in a magic chair and demands another seven years as the price of freedom. Millicent Clare loves her fiancé, Raymond Mounchensey; but her father Sir Arthur, unsure of Sir Richard Mounchensey’s financial stability, plans to break off the match and place her as a novice in a nunnery for three months while he arranges an alternative marriage with young Jerningham. Feeling sorry for Raymond, his friend and one-time fellow student, Fabell, tells him what Sir Arthur has in mind, and the young men plot to frustrate the old man’s plans. Millicent is entered in the nunnery, and is dismayed at her new life of abstinence, prayer, and flagellation. Posing as the Abbot of Waltham, Fabell substitutes Raymond for one of the friars, Benedick. Taking him for the novice he seems, Sir Arthur tells Raymond everything, and takes him to hear Millicent’s confession. This enables him to plan Millicent’s escape. The young men succeed in getting Millicent out of the nunnery, but are separated from her when they encounter some locals on a poaching expedition: assuming them to be Sir Arthur, they lead the locals off the scent. Millicent is found by the keeper. The poachers rendezvous in Enfield churchyard and scare the sexton into thinking he has seen a ghost. Sir Arthur complains to the real Abbot of Waltham about Millicent’s abduction, but is discomfited when the Abbot produces the real Friar Benedick. Sir Arthur and Sir Richard return to their inn, but spend the night in the wrong one after the signs are switched. In their absence, Raymond and Millicent are married. Sir Arthur accepts the fait accompli.
