Well, sirs, take heed what you do, I am a Princes man, if you stay me upon the Kings hie way, I can lay fell lowship to your charge. Without the word! Oh pernicious Frenchman! without the word! why, I have called thee Villain, him Rascal; this, Slave; that, Rogue; and am I still without the word? Faith, you're Like to watch this twelvemonth, ere you have any other words at my hands. I came, sir, from the King of France's camp. My name, sir, is Bow wow. No dog, sir, would you should know it, though I be encompassed with curs. Not you, sir, I call not you so: I know you to be a very insufficient ill-spoken Gentleman. My master, sir, is the Lady Katharine, the French Kings daughter: I have been abroad about some business of hers, and am now going back again. Stand of: but soft, I do not know your name, sir, that my Lady may give you thanks. Then, master Dick Bowyer, after my hearty commendations, endue: but as for the rest, I shall, I say no more, I shall— 'Tis true, they are gone together, and I am sent to watch Prince Ferdinand, and the Earl of Pembroke: this way they went; but they are God out of sighed. I were very bade to be a Hound, that have no better a sent: if they were hares, as they are men, I should think them squatted: but hushed, here comes one of them. Here comes the tother, this is luck upon luck: now will I run and fetch my mistress, the Lady Katharine, to partly their fray. Just in this circle I left the two Princes ready to draw; for I read the whole discourse of the Combat in their read eyes. Have they not slain one another, and buried themselves? Oh Lord, then let me turn my self into a Ballad, and mourn for them. Go from your sighed? then let me go out of your company: for I had as leave leave your sighed as your company. Is this my reward for watching and watch king? O Mistress, do not kill me with unkindness: I shall, I shall— Weep out mine eyes, and fill the holes with salt water. Well, I were best be going, now I am so fairly offered. Mistress, your reward hath stopped my tears, and en tic'de my legs to be walking. Farewell, I will go, God knows whither, to seek and to find both, and neither. Farewell, sweet Mistress. Come on, Madam, me thinks now a mask would do well: but I perceive your drift, I smell your policy, you think a bald face hath no needs of a black mask. Shall I tell you what you look Like? A broiled herring, or a tortur'de Image made of plaster work. Out of peradventure, Madam, the ghost of Saint George is come out of England, to see what hospitality S. Denis keeps in France. Antic! thou liest, and thou Wertenberg a Knight of gingerbread: I am no Antic, the whole parish where I was born will swear, that since the reign of Charlemain there was not a better face bred or brought up amongst them. Is my young Lord buried here? I say no more, but I pray God send him a joyful insurrection. Do not think, Madam, that isle forsake you. And so, sir, you that walk in pewter vessayle, Like one of the wor thighs, will you be ruled by me? To set a gyn for Woodcocks, & catch your self first. By your leave, sweet bloods: may a Gentleman or so, deceive two or three ounces of words in this assembly? Is there not a young Kings son amongst you, who treading the steps of his father, is called Philip? Then the old saying is verified, He that seeks, shall find. Here is a poor kinswoman of mine would desire some private conference with you, or so.

Plot summary

Plot — The Trial of Chivalry (This Gallant Cavaliero Dick Bowyer)
France is on the point of battle over its claim to suzerainty over Navarre, but the children of the two royal families call for peace: Ferdinand and Philip love Katharine and Bellamira. A truce of three months is agreed; if the marriages take place, the war will end permanently. Neither courtship is successful, however. Katharine is in love with the Earl of Pembroke, Navarre’s ally, and treats Ferdinand with disdain. Ferdinand appoints Pembroke to woo her on his behalf, but her behaviour afterwards convinces him that Pembroke has wooed her on his own account. He challenges Pembroke to a duel; they wound one another, and are reported dead. Meanwhile, the French traitor Bourbon aims to secure the crown of Navarre for himself by marrying Bellamira, then poisoning Ferdinand and the King. Balked by the planned diplomatic marriage and rejected by Bellamira herself, he uses a poison supplied by his ally, Duke Roderick, to disfigure Bellamira’s face. Philip insists that he will marry her anyway, but she runs away before the wedding can take place. Duke Roderick reports untruthfully that Katharine too has run away after being raped by Ferdinand. France accepts Bourbon’s submission and offer of troops; both kingdoms are back on a war footing. In fact, unknown to each other, both Ferdinand and Pembroke have been found by countrymen and nursed back to health. Believing Ferdinand dead, Pembroke erects a tomb to him and demands that passers-by leave their shields in tribute. Katharine visits the tomb and repents her ill usage of Ferdinand. When Ferdinand himself arrives, Pembroke tries to force him too to leave his shield; they fight, but stop when they recognize one another. Having told Katharine that he has commissioned a funerary statue for the tomb, Pembroke gets Ferdinand to take its place when she next visits, and so brings them together. Philip seeks revenge on Bourbon, and gets help from Roderick. On the eve of battle, Philip meets Bourbon incognito and kills him, but Roderick then pretends not to recognize Philip and attempts to kill him as a murderer. Pembroke and Ferdinand rescue Philip, and the three of them join the battle. After Philip kills Roderick, they stop the fighting and explain to France and Navarre that the war has been brought about by the slander and treachery of Roderick and Bourbon. The marriage alliances are revived, and peace is declared.
