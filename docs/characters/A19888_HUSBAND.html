Full speech

Why Nan, asleep or dead? Why dost not open door? Why dost not rise? I'm almost starved, prithee make hast. I fear no robbing, and for my Carcase, I still bear About me to defend that, I've traveled hard to day, And am very hungry prithee wife see what thou hast in the house? Talk not to me, something I must and will have. Cold troubles me, lay me some Faggots on. I'll have't revived. Now bless us wife, what noise is in the Garret? Thanks good Friar Bernard, Now as I live, would I had some good cheer, No better then I would pay for; but she tells me There's nothing to be compassed. But any fare that would content the stomach. But Friar John, how shall we come by meat? Can Friar John Conjure? Nan, when I say? Nor any here? Would we might see them. Are you in earnest? Two fine and delicate Manchets. I shall. Faith I say, would I might see't. Most admirable, see, here's a Bottle full. Excellent stuff, I never drank better Clarret. Any thing. Nothing better. See how she stirs? I would have had some company, I had not Such and appetite to be merry for and hour this seven year; That I could tell where to call up some good fellow That I knew, we would not partly these two hours. Bed me no beds, I'll know the reason of't. Dost thou know them? Friar Bernard, and Friar John ? only good Lads That I desired to meet with; I beshrew thee They had no better welcome; go, unlock, Entreat them hither, we will have one cup To health of all their Covent. Time enough, early betimes, nay, quick Nan, Friar John there? The bonniest Lad ever wore portace in a string, Or mumbled Mass, or Matins; I but wished And see, it's come to pass; the other to, Though he be strict of life, yet will sometime Be merry, if he Like his company; But my kind honest baldpate, Friar Jack, Enter woman and Friars. I shall be glad to see him; welcome faith, With reverence, Father, to your gravity, Be not offended if Friar John and I Be, not exceeding compass, a little merry, And play with the Pot; fill some in. Bred and Drink? how shall we come by Meat? I, but where? Marry Nan, my Wife. A hight fat Pullet, newly dressed and sauced. I never herd the Like, Good wine, good bred, good victuals, stomach good, And all to meet together? nay fallen to, And he be thanked that sent it. I pray Friar John, what spirit do you deal with? And what's he? Could you not show your Devil for a needs, To one that feign would see him? Why in his own. Yet would I see him. With all my heart. Yes, twenty I could name. No shape better. Why he's my honest Gossip. Why as my friend, my Neighbour, and my Gossip. Fear not that, If kicks and spurns will drive the Devil hence, I'll help to send him packing. Will kicks do't? Let me alone to conjure him, pox on the Devil, He hath put me in a sweat. Yes, do good Nan ; I think Friar John I have bumbasted the Devil; thanks for our Supper, French wine, and Spanish Loaves, I never tasted meat That more contented me. Good night Friar John, and holy Father Bernard.

Plot summary

Plot — A New Trick to Cheat the Devil
Anne Changeable wants to marry Slightall, with her father’s consent, but her mother persuades her that marriage to Lord Scales will be better for her social standing. Slightall takes rejection badly: he turns to misogyny and voluptuous hedonism. One of his servants, Roger, tries unsuccessfully to dissuade him from squandering his money, while the other, Geoffrey, helps make an arrangement with a usurer to supply him with a further £1,000 — the usurer’s objective being to acquire his land. Lord Scales visits Anne, and she finds that he is not a good kisser: Slightall is the better catch in every respect save his lack of a title; but when she tries to reactivate the romance, Slightall rejects her. He has been ruined by prodigality and pays off his servants; Geoffrey is taken on by Lord Scales, but Roger declines a similar offer. Anne insults everyone who persuaded her to jilt Slightall, but this only makes Lord Scales more attracted to her. Changeable promises Anne that he will get Slightall back for her. Driven out of his wits with melancholy, Slightall conjures up Beelzebub, and makes a contract: the devil will give him enough money to pay his debts in exchange for the right to take his soul when he no longer owes anyone anything. On the day his debt to the usurer falls due, he turns out to have ample money to pay, so the usurer gets his money back rather than the lands he coveted. Asked by Anne to give Slightall some financial support, Lord Scales finds his offer rejected with contempt. The Changeables’ house is troubled by a female spirit; Slightall agrees to help exorcise it by spending the night in the haunted room. When he does so, he is visited by the ghost in Anne’s form. Beelzebub comes for Slightall’s soul, but Slightall demonstrates that he is not yet clear of debt: he paid the usurer with money loaned by Beelzebub. The devil accepts the situation and offers to marry him to the she-devil who haunts the Changeables’ house. Anne’s mother and Lord Scales give their consent, and watch as a friar performs the ceremony. In fact it is not a ghost in Anne’s form, but Anne herself; and ‘Beelzebub’ was really Changeable: Anne has finally got her man back. In the sub-plot, two friars are detained until late by charitable visitation, and find themselves locked out of their abbey. They ask Nan to give them a bed for the night.
