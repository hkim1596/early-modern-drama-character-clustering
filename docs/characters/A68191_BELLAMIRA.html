For Bellamiraes sake abstain from war. Gentle Philip. Here I am, who calls on Bellamira? The Duke of Burbon in my Tent so late? Where is my Guard? what Peter, Thomasin. Must I? You will not force me to it? Poison my face! o most inhumane wretch! Revenge more vile, then to abridge my life. What, Thomasin, what, brother Ferdinand: My kingly father: is there none that hears? Then Treason, treason, let that waken you: For capital is this offence to me. My Philip, o give leave to fly his sighed. Deny me, father, I would not see Prince Philip with this face. Oh let me leave the presence, gentle father, When Philip bids his Bellamira go. He came into my Tent at dead of night, And rubbed my face with and infectuous herb, Because I would not grant unto his love. I cried for help, but none did succour me. Marry with me? fetch me a looking glass, That I may see how sweet a Bride I am. O, I detest my self: Dear, hate me too: I am not to be married but to death. Though I were Empress of the spacious world, I de lay my self and Kingdom at thy feet. Live, noble Philip, joy some happier match: 'Tis my unworthiness makes me deny thee. Now, Bellamira, thou hast time to think Upon these troublous matters: should I suffer So brave a Gentleman as Philip is, To wed himself to my unworthy self, It would be counted virtue in the Prince; But I were worthy of a world of blame. No, Philip, no, thou shalt not wrong thine honour, Nor be impeached by Bellamiraes spots. In some disguise isle steal away to night, And never appear more in my Philips sighed. For Philips sake I have been martyred thus, And for his sake left King and Courtly life, To entertain a Pilgrim's painful habit. But on what strange adventure stays this Knight, Within this desolate forsaken wood? Is this my brother's Hearse? is this the place, Where I was shipwracked of a brother's name? O, let me spend a loving sighs for him, And sacrifice a sisters holy rites: For ever rest, sweet Ferdinand, in peace, Until thy body glorified from heaven, Become immortal by thy souls return. My Lord of Burbon, you presume too much On extremity of passion. Have I not answered many and idle letter, With full assurance that I cannot love? Have I not often, viva voice checked Your courtly kindness? frowned upon your smiles? Used you unkindly, all to wean your love? And do you still persever in your suit? I tell thee, Burbon, this bold partly of thine, To break into my Tent at dead of night, Deserves severe correction, and the more, Because it brings mine honour into question. I charge thee, as thou art a Gentleman, Betake thee to thine own Pavilion, And let this answer satisfy for all: Burbon, I cannot, nor I will not love thee. Burbon, o Burbon. So, sirrah, you may scoff my misery.

Plot summary

Plot — The Trial of Chivalry (This Gallant Cavaliero Dick Bowyer)
France is on the point of battle over its claim to suzerainty over Navarre, but the children of the two royal families call for peace: Ferdinand and Philip love Katharine and Bellamira. A truce of three months is agreed; if the marriages take place, the war will end permanently. Neither courtship is successful, however. Katharine is in love with the Earl of Pembroke, Navarre’s ally, and treats Ferdinand with disdain. Ferdinand appoints Pembroke to woo her on his behalf, but her behaviour afterwards convinces him that Pembroke has wooed her on his own account. He challenges Pembroke to a duel; they wound one another, and are reported dead. Meanwhile, the French traitor Bourbon aims to secure the crown of Navarre for himself by marrying Bellamira, then poisoning Ferdinand and the King. Balked by the planned diplomatic marriage and rejected by Bellamira herself, he uses a poison supplied by his ally, Duke Roderick, to disfigure Bellamira’s face. Philip insists that he will marry her anyway, but she runs away before the wedding can take place. Duke Roderick reports untruthfully that Katharine too has run away after being raped by Ferdinand. France accepts Bourbon’s submission and offer of troops; both kingdoms are back on a war footing. In fact, unknown to each other, both Ferdinand and Pembroke have been found by countrymen and nursed back to health. Believing Ferdinand dead, Pembroke erects a tomb to him and demands that passers-by leave their shields in tribute. Katharine visits the tomb and repents her ill usage of Ferdinand. When Ferdinand himself arrives, Pembroke tries to force him too to leave his shield; they fight, but stop when they recognize one another. Having told Katharine that he has commissioned a funerary statue for the tomb, Pembroke gets Ferdinand to take its place when she next visits, and so brings them together. Philip seeks revenge on Bourbon, and gets help from Roderick. On the eve of battle, Philip meets Bourbon incognito and kills him, but Roderick then pretends not to recognize Philip and attempts to kill him as a murderer. Pembroke and Ferdinand rescue Philip, and the three of them join the battle. After Philip kills Roderick, they stop the fighting and explain to France and Navarre that the war has been brought about by the slander and treachery of Roderick and Bourbon. The marriage alliances are revived, and peace is declared.
