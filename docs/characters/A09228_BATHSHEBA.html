Come gentle Zephire tricked with those perfumes That erst in Eden sweetened Adam's love, And stroke my bosom with the silken fan: This shade (sun proof) is yet no proof for thee, Thy body smoother then this waveless spring, And purer then the substance of the same, Can creep through that his lances cannot Pierce. Thou and thy sister soft and sacred air, Goddess of life and governess of health, Keeps every fountain fresh and arbour sweet, No brazen gate, her passage can repulse, Nor bushly thicket, bar thy subtle breath, Then deck thee with thy lose delightsome robes, And on thy wings bring delicate perfumes, To play the wantons with us through the leaves, Ah what is Bethsheeba to please the King, Or what is David, that he should desire For fickle beauties sake his servants wife? My lord the King, elect to Gods own heart, Should not his gracious jealousy incense, Whose thoughts are chased, I hate incontinence. The Kings poor handmaid will obey my lord, One medicine cannot heal our different harms. But rather make both rankle at the bone, Then let the King be cunning in his cure, Least flattering both, both perish in his hand. Mourn Bethsheeba, bewail thy foolishness, Thy sin, thy shame, the sorrow of thy soul, Sin, shame, and sorrow swarm about thy soul, And in the gates and entrance of my heart, Sadness with wreathed arms hangs her complaint. The Due that on the hill of Hermon falls, Reins not on Zions tops, and lofty towers, And David's thoughts are spent in pensiveness, The plains of Gath and Askaron rejoice. The babe is sick, sweet babe, that Bersabe With woman's pain brought forth to Israel. Enter Nathan. But what says Nathan to his lord the king? What means my lord, the lamp of Israel, From whose bright eyes all eyes receive their light, To dim the glory of his sweet aspects, And paint his countenance with his hearts distress? Why should his thoughts retain a sad conceit, When every pleasure kneels before his throne, And sues for sweet acceptance with his grace, Take but your Lute, and make the mountains dance, Retrieve the suns sphere, and restrain the clouds, Give ears to trees, make savage Lyons tame, Impose still silence to the loudest winds, And fill the fairest day with foulest storms, Then why should passion of much meaner power, Bear head against the heart of Israel. Then is the pleasure of my sovereigns heart, So wrapped within the bosom of that son, That Salomon, whom Israel's God affects, And gave the name unto him for his love, Should be no salve to comfort David's soul? Now sits thy sorrows sucking of my blood, Oh that it might be poison to their powers, And that their lips might draw my bosom dry, So David's love might ease him, though she die. Plot summary

Plot — The Love of David and Fair Bathsheba
PLOT King David sees Bathsheba bathing and falls in love, but she resists his advances. To check his desires, he recalls her husband, Uriah, from the war, where his army has just conquered a town. Uriah returns, refuses to see his wife, and becomes embarrassingly drunk at a royal feast. David sends him back to the war, with orders that he should be placed in the vanguard. Uriah is killed in battle, removing the obstacle to David's liaison with Bathsheba; but he is rebuked by the prophet Nathan and the child of their union falls sick and dies. David believes that this removes Bathsheba's shame, and enables him to legitimize their relationship and beget another child. Amnon falls in love with his half-sister Tamar and feigns sickness in order to be alone with her. He rapes her and then throws her out. She complains to her brother Absalom, who plots revenge. Despite David's injunction to leave justice to the crown, Absalom goes to Amnon's pastoral feast and has him murdered. Now that Israel is about to conquer the Ammonite enemy, Joab asks David to come to the war, so that the triumph will belong to him rather than to his general. The Israelites are celebrating their victory when David hears of Amnon's murder. He banishes Absalom. A widow tells him how she stands to lose both sons because one murdered the other; he pronounces in favour of the surviving son, then applies the case to his own circumstances and recalls Absalom — which is what Joab intended in sending the woman to him. Absalom becomes ambitious and rises in rebellion. His associate Ahitophel encourages him to send men to kill David by night, but Hushai advises raising a much larger army so that he can overwhelm David by force of numbers; Absalom prefers Hushai's advice. In fact, Hushai is a spy seeking to drive a wedge between Absalom and Ahitophel; he reports Ahitophel's assassination plans to David, and advises him to withdraw out of harm's way across the Jordan. Having lost his influence with Absalom, Ahitophel hangs himself. The rebellion is crushed in battle. Absalom gets his hair caught in a tree as he flees, and is trapped hanging between heaven and earth. Although David has ordered that Absalom be spared, Joab kills him where he hangs. David is distressed, but takes comfort in the new heir he has begotten on Bathsheba, Solomon.
