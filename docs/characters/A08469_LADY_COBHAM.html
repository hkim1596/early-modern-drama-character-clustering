You are welcome home, my Lord, Why seem you so disquiet in your looks? What hath befallen you that disquiets your mind? So soon my Lord? what will you ride all night? My husband hight with travelling my lord, His nose gushed out a bleeding, that was it. To cut such simple victual as we had. I prithee Harpoole, look unto thy Lord, I do not Like this sudden posting back. Come madam, and my lord, weel hope the best, You shall not into Wales till he return. What murder? you upbraid us wrongfully. My Lord, believe me, as I have a soul, I know not where my lord my husband is. My husband is a noble gentleman, And need not hide himself for any fact That ere I herd of, therefore wrong him not. Oh God requite thee thou bloodthirsty man. But where (my Lord,) Shall we find rest for our disquiet minds? There dwell untamed thoughts that hardly stoop, To such abasemen of disdained rags, We were not wont to travel thus by night, Especially on foot. How can it seem a trouble, having you A partner with me, in the worst I feel? No gentle Lord, your presence would give ease To death it self, should he now seize upon me, Behold what my foresight hath undertaken heres bred and cheese & a bottle. For fear we faint, they are but homely cates. Yet sau de with hunger, they may seem as sweet, As greater dainties we were wont to taste. And I incoragde with your cheerful speech, Will do the Like. Fear not my Lord, he's witty to devise, And strong to execute a present shift. Let that depend on me, while you do sleep, I'll watch that no misfortune happen us, Lay then your head upon my lap sweet Lord, And boldly take your rest. Urge not that, My duty binds me, and your love commands. I would I had the skill with tuned voice, To draw on sleep with some sweet melody, But imperfectoin and unaptness too, Are both repugnant, fear inserts the one, The other nature hath denied me use. But what talk I of means to purchase that, Is freely happened? sleep with gentle hand, Hath shut his eyelids, o victorious labour, How soon thy power can charm the bodies sense? And now thou likewise climbst unto my brain, Making my heavy temples stoop to thee, Great God of heaven from danger keep us free. both sleeps. Say (gentle Lord) for now we are alone, And may confer, shall we confess in brief, Of whence, and what we are, and so prevent The accusation is commenede against us? Well, be it then according as heaven please. Oh my deer Lord, what shall betide of us? You to the Tower, and I turned out of doors, Our substance ceased unto his highness use, Even to the garments longing to our backs.

Plot summary

Plot — 1 Sir John Oldcastle
Despite a royal prohibition on disorder during the preparations for the invasion of France, Lords Herbert and Po·wys fall out over their religious differences, and brawl with their followers in the streets of Hereford;Herbert is wounded, and Powys flees, with orders given for his arrest. The Bishop of Rochester does his best to associate dissent and conspiracy with Sir John Oldcastle, but the King is not convinced. Powys takes refuge in Oldcastle's house, just before two orders arrive: one to attend at court, the other to answer charges before Rochester's ecclesiastical court; Oldcastle's servant Harpoole forces Rochester's summoner to eat his own summons, wax and all. The King advises Oldcastle to keep his religious views private: he can believe what he likes so long as he does not antagonize the bishops or encourage Protestant rebellion. In turn. Oldcastle intercedes for Powys, and the King signs his pardon. Rebellion is brewing in England. On the basis of a superior dynastic claim, the Earl of Cambridge intends to seize the throne with French support. Oldcastle is invited to join the plot, but he procures an incriminating document and takes it to court. Meanwhile, Protestant rebels muster their forces in London. The King plans to spy on them in disguise, but is robbed by the renegade priest Sir John; the priest gets him to promise to secure the King's pardon for robbery, and gives him half a coin as a token by which they can know one another again. On the night before the battle, the King. in his own person, plays dice with Sir John and wins back his stolen money, including the broken coin; as promised, he agrees to spare Sir John's life ifhe gives up his life of crime. The rebels are routed, but the prisoners falsely implicate Oldcastle. The charges collapse when it becomes clear that the rebels don't recognize him, and Oldcastle further demonstrates his loyalty with his evidence of the Cambridge conspiracy. The rebel leaders are executed, Cambridge exposed and arrested, and the King's faith in Oldcastle is restored. In the King's absence, Rochester has Oldcastle's property seized, and Oldcastle himself arrested for treason when he arrives home; his books are burnt when they are found to be in English. Rochester visits Oldcastle in prison in the hope that he may recant, but Oldcastle overpowers him and escapes in his clothes. On the run, he arrives with his wife in St Albans, but is forced to escape arrest by another illicit exchange of clothes.…
