Happy be your arrival, Noble friends; You are the first, that Like to Doves repair To my new building; you are my firstborn guests, My eldest sons of hospitality; Here's to my hearty welcomes. 'Tis finished, and the cost stands on no score, None can for want of payment, at my door, Curse my foundation, praying the roof may fallen On the proud builders head, seeing the smoke go Out of those Chimneys, for whose bricks I owe. Pray call it not so: The humble shrub, no Cedar here shall grow; You see Three hundred Doric pillars stand About one square, Three hundred Noble friends Laid (in their loves) at raising of those Columns, A piece of gold under each Pedestal, With his name graved upon the bottom stone, Except that cost, all other was mine own; See here, each days expenses are so great, They make a volume, for in this appears, It was no task of weeks, or months, but years: I trust my steward only with the key, Which keeps that secret; here's Arithmetic For churls to cast up, there's nhe root of all; If you have skill in numbers, number that. To what? Thou vain vainglorious fool, go burn that Book, No Herald needs to blazon Charities Arms; Go burn it presently. Away, I launch not forth a ship, with drums and guns, And Trumpets, to proclaim my gallantry; He that will read the wasting of my gold, Shall find it writ in ashes, which the wind Will scatter ere he spends it; Another day, The wheel may turn, and I that built thus high, May by the storms of want, be driven to dwell In a thatched Cottage; Rancour shall not then Spit poison at me, pinning on my back This carded; He that spent thus much, now does lack. My gates fill up the number of seven days, At which, of guests, seven several sorts I'll welcome: On Monday, Knights whose Fortune's are sunk low; On Tuesday, those that all their life-long read The huge voluminous wonders of the deep, Seamen (I mean) and so on other days, Others shall take their turns. For the years twelve moons; In each of which, twelve Tables shall be spread; At them, such whom the world scorns, shall be fed, The windows of my building, which each morn, Are Porters, to let in men comfort (light) Are numbered just three hundred sixty five, And in so many days the sun does drive His chariot stuck with beams of Burnished gold, My Alms shall such diurnal progress make As doe's the sun in his bright- Zodiac. Such Lords eat men, but men shall eat up me, My uncle the Lord Abbot had a soul Subtle and quick, and searching as the fire, By Magic-stayers he went as deep as hell, And if in devils possession gold be kept, He brought some sure from thence, it's hid in caves Known (save to me) to none, and Like a spring The more it's drawn, the more it still does rise, The more my heap wastes, more it multiplies. Now whither (as most rich-men do) he pawned His soul for that deer purchase none can tell, But by his bedside when he saw death stand Fetching a deep groan, me he catch't-by'th hand Called me his heir, and charged me well to spend What he had God ill, deal (quoth he) a dole which round (with good men's prayers) may guard my soul Now at her setting forth: let none feel want That knock but at thy gates: do wrong to none, And what request to thee so ear is made, If honest, see it never be denayed. Fair & upright, As are the strict vows of and Anchorite: A benefit given by a Niggards hand Is and gravily bred, the hunger-sterv'd Takes it, but cannot eat it; I'll give none such. Who with free heart shakes out but crumbs, gives much. A wife? when I shall have one hand in heaven, To write my happiness in leaves of stars; A wife wo'd pluck me by the other down: This Bark hath thus long sailed about the world, My soul the Pilot, and yet never listened To such a Mare-maids song: a wife, o fetters, To men blessed liberty! All this world's a prison, Heaven the high wall about it, sin the jalour, But the iron-shackles weighing down our heals, Are only women, those light Angels turn us, To fleshly devils, I that Sex admire, But never will fit near their wanton fire. My heirs shall be poor children fed on alms, Soldiers that want limbs, scholars poor and scorned. And these will be a sure inheritance; Not to decay: Manors and Towns will fallen, Lordships and Parks, Pastures and woods be sold; But this Land still continues to the Lord: No subtle tricks of law, can me beguile of this. But of the beggars-dish, I shall drink healths To last for ever; whilst I live, my roof Shall cover naked wretches; when I die, It's dedicated to St. Charity. Plot summary

Plot — The Wonder of A Kingdom
The Duke of Florence has betrothed his daughter, Fiametta, to the Prince of Pisa, and, to facilitate the political marriage, banished her lover, Angelo. Fiametta sickens for her absent love, and he returns to court disguised as a French doctor, offering the Duke his services to save her life. He diagnoses sexual frustration, and proposes a cure which requires the patient to drink a potion made from the beloved’s powdered heart: she will then hate him. In order to obtain the key ingredient, Angelo must be murdered. Angelo reveals his presence to Fiametta, but she insists on identifying him to her father, in the expectation that the Duke will relent. Angelo passes this off as an insane delusion, then flees to sanctuary. He is found and brought to court with a friar. He seems to reject Fiametta, and she agrees to marry Pisa the following day, but asks for the friar to hear her confession overnight. In fact, he secretly marries her to Angelo, pre-empting Pisa; the Duke and Prince are annoyed, but recognize there is nothing to be done about it. In the sub-plot, Lord Vanni has amorous inclinations younger than his physical age, and sends a love-letter and jewel to the chaste Alphonsina Neri. Meanwhile, Alphonsina’s brother Tibaldo has fallen sick for love of Vanni’s wife Dariene. He asks Alphonsina to accept Vanni’s advances, and Vanni duly excuses himself from Dariene’s bed. Tibaldo has arranged for himself to be present disguised as Alphonsina’s sister, and offers himself as an alternative, ‘female’ bedfellow for Dariene. His plan is aborted when he learns that Vanni’s daughter Alisandra loves Trebatio and Trebatio’s son. When Vanni pays his nocturnal visit, he is scared off by Trebatio’s lute-playing, then caught by his wife and blamed for the adultery he never committed. Alphonsina and Tibaldo marry Trebatio and Alisandra. In the third plot, the hospitable Jacomo has built a house in which to entertain his guests: his uncle, who is suspected of getting rich through supernatural agency, gave him a deathbed injunction to spend his inheritance virtuously. Torrenti envies Jacomo his new house, and plans a magnificent lifestyle of his own; but whereas Jacomo entertains the poor and needy, Torrenti is only interested in luxury and guests of a high social standing.
