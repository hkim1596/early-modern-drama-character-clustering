Ene as you please, Madam, both either, or neither. I think not Madam, 'tis some young Prentice Your old shoemaker of Feversham hath God. I have seen a worse face in better clothes. He would do well if he knew the true length Of your foot Madam. I will: If you doubt call me back. Exit Nurse. Come sir, you must answer a default to my Lady. And why was the creak of shoes first devised sir? Were thy Parents of this profession? He speaks well, sure there is some hard characters which I Understand not: I Like not these shoes, you must make and easier partly? Shall I call the shoemaker again Lady? Nay I know not Madam. Madam why chide you me? I will not love your shoemaker. Does he not make your shoes? therefore your shoemaker: But think not Lady I can be so base being so near your presence, To love such a groom, if but for your credit sake. Yet that's no president for me, I have known Ladies remove Their stable grooms into their bedchambers, and lower offices then That too: o'twas a policy, and hereafter may be in fashion for Great Ladies to match with their inferiors, because the woman Adding no dignity to the man, nor losing her own, still keeps Supremacy, he waits as dutiful on her trencher as when He was her servant. Twenty of mine own knowledge, that I have seen in my days. my closet, then thou shalt tell me some, if thou P again, if I find president I'll Follow it, (I'll else begin my self,) if there be none Let after Ladies coat me down for one. Exeunt. Good faith I am weary Madam, I never knew my tongue Would tire before: you have not let me close mine eyes to night. But I am old, when I was young, love would have kept me waking. A Scriveners son, no better verily, there's many Bonds yet in Room canceled, where he's subscri'd a public notary. Not at all Madam, nay I could come nearer were I sure it Would not offend your Grace. As I have herd great Maximinus Father, your Father's Father Madam, was but even a Smith, that with his labour Hammered out his living. It's now my every morning's work to watch the coming of Your shoemaker: pray Venus my Lady tread not her shoes awry, She changes her shoes so often. Exit Nurse. Anon sweet mouse. Yet more work for your Shoemaker, well, well, You play the wag, and I the lie must tell. I fear me there's a shoe wrings her i'th instep, of my young Shoemakers making: such fellows as he cannot choose but be slippery companions; for first they know the length of a Ladies foot, and then they have such tricks to smooth her shoe, and tickle her sole; as I protest, if I were a Shoemaker my self, it would make my teeth water: what a sweet thing it is, to have a round sweet, plump, de licate Calve of a Ladies leg lie rolling on his thigh, while he lies smoothing her fine silk stocking, slips his hand to her garters, and sometime higher byr'lady; I have been served so my self: there's ma nigh a Gallant, I can tell you, would give all the shoes in's shop to have a shoemakers office in a morning: Well, well, I say nothing, but I suspect something: Pity of me, she's as broad behind as I am, and round enough before: I doubt me he has made her a pair of short-heeled shoes with a turn-over: Come sweet Mouse, have you given instructions to your Shoemaker? Why what a fellow art thou, canst not find a Last to fit her yet? Out of my way Sir: o my heart! The matter say ye? pray let me gape alittle; was out of my wits before with fear, and now for joy. O my heart, I think in my conscience I have not so much wind left in my belly as will blow out a Candle: The Princess, the Princess Sir. Oh my sweet Lambkin's found, And come to Court too. A pretty handsome stripling by my Holydame; Her own Shoemaker belike, poor duckling: She was wandering, and he met with her; And belike she had worn out her shoes, and he fitted her finely: So drew on her shoes first, and drew her to Court after; And he and all the Company of the Gentle Craft Sir, Brings her home most sumptuously. This is the youth that doubles they: Oh my sweet Honey- uckle, have I found thee again? Nay, I was asleep when 'twas done i'faith. Plot summary

Plot — A Shoemaker a Gentleman
The Roman Emperors, Diocletian and Maximinus, bring an army to Britain to crush the Christians. Alured, the British King, is killed in battle. His sons, Eldred and Offa, change their identities and get themselves apprenticed to a shoemaker. They see their mother taken to prison after being captured on the battlefield; their sympathy for her plight impresses their new master. Alban is appointed to rule Britain and continue the persecution. Amphiabel comes from Wales and converts him to Christianity. Maximinus arrests Alban, and Amphiabel flees back to Wales, pursued by the Roman army. Meanwhile, the Welsh Queen Winifred, also converted by Amphiabel, asks her suitor Sir Hugh for a moratorium on his wooing efforts; when he completes the agreed period, however, she announces that she has given herself wholly to heaven and cannot marry him. One of the Romans pursuing Amphiabel mocks the power of Winifred’s holy well, and smears the water on his eyes; it blinds him. His sight is restored, but he ascribes the miracle to the pagan gods. Winifred and Amphiabel are both arrested and brought to see the martyrdom of Alban. Leodice, Maximinus’ daughter, becomes enamoured of her new shoemaker, Crispin, who is really Offa; she continually finds reasons to asks for new shoes and new fitting sessions. She offers to show him the image of his future wife in a magic mirror; but in fact it is an ordinary mirror, and he sees her reflection. He confides that he is not really base-born, but a disguised prince, and they are secretly married. The Romans are mustering an army to resist an invasion of the Empire by Goths and Vandals. Eldred volunteers, and the shoemaker employs Sir Hugh to fill his place. Eldred distinguishes himself in battle: he saves Diocletian’s life, takes back the eagle standard after it has been seized by the enemy, and captures the Vandal king Roderick. The barbarians are expelled, and Diocletian knights Eldred. Sir Hugh is arrested after he sees Winifred a prisoner and reviles the Roman gods. Alban and Amphiabel are tortured in an attempt to make them renounce Christianity, but they remain steadfast and are sent for execution; Alban becomes Britain’s first Christian martyr ~ but not the last. Winifred is allowed to choose the manner of her own execution; she opts to bleed to death. Sir Hugh is made to drink her blood, mixed with poison.
