A Way, stand off, prop not a falling Castle with Your weak strength, 'tis sinful Charity, and Desperate folly to meet a mischief, whose Entertainment is assured destruction: leave me I pray safeguard your own lives. You tire me out of breath with vain delays, as well May you give life unto a stone, a senseless statue; My life's but lent to bid you shun your death's, and in that too Heavens mercy is miraculous, yet you will not hear me: Again I charge you as a King; yet none regards Declining Majesty; then as a husband, and a Father here; Dost thou love me? Have you duty, you Phoenix of my age, for though Two persons be distinguishable, yet ought there be but one Combined heart in your fraternal union, your knees promise. Then here I charge ye for to leave the Field, Fly from death, he's now in persuite of ye: Fly from the Tyrant, for this unhappy day Those bloody Persecutors Maximinus, and Dioclesian, Display their by neckt Eagle over Britain, While she lies under as a bleeding prey, One Talon here is fastened. Ene one the Virge of Bliss, Oh deer Amphiabell. Noble Sir Hugh, what more could I have wished, then breath To thank your kind assistance in this hapless day: O take and equal jonyter with my Sons, from this cold Oracle All I bequeathe is counsel for your safety, fly the slaughter, For dying men are half Prophetical, if you abide A longer stay you fallen: o do not make me guilty of your death's That drew you hither to expire your breathes, this path I Progress but avoid my way, you need not haste To and assured danger: Farewell my love, my blessing here Shall fallen, perform my will, else Fate avert it all: Thou canst not boast grim death: I did not yield, Nor fell by Agues, but Like a King ith' field. Albon shall still as substitute to Room Observe, and keep her high imperial Doom. Thou constant friend what title shall I give, endue to thy merit; It's more then a friendly partly to fetch from hell, friends turn At misery, they abide long that partly at prison doors, the Best lasts but till death, but thou hast stood the renovation of A second life; what may be given him for and epethite That of a tyrant makes a profelite? I am your poor Disciple, my tutor Mr. but friend I call you by No other name, although I derogate from your Princely Office Cause I will follow you, if not proceed, ever through the Jaws of persecution; I will not trust your person to the danger: Let this help your haste, although Prince of Wales, yet in the Course you take you may chance needs This drudging God of fools to help you speed. Let me approve without a boast, the Actium of my love; this in the Open face of tyranny, I'll daring wear: and in approbation of Such and Altar, Sacrifice my blood: but sweet friend hear of me, Behold it not: I wo'd not have you lost. Put me in your prayers, that mercies white hand may cross The debt book wherein I stand above my height in gore, this Hand hath done and act of bloody persecution. Here Maxeminus. But I am and English man. Not. Not persecuting, but Christian Albon. Yes Emperor, read in this book, if by this abstract Thou canst understand, the Volume is within. You'll search too late. Gone, I have convaide him hence. No, I have deserved to feel a Tyrants sword, because my sword Was glazed in tyranny; I am in debt for blood, make thou it even, Tyrants and fiends are officers to heaven. Kill me first, or I shall spurn thine Idol. I am blessed in curses, now Albon shall be tried, Man is gold oar, when he is purified Exit Alb.

Plot summary

Plot — A Shoemaker a Gentleman
The Roman Emperors, Diocletian and Maximinus, bring an army to Britain to crush the Christians. Alured, the British King, is killed in battle. His sons, Eldred and Offa, change their identities and get themselves apprenticed to a shoemaker. They see their mother taken to prison after being captured on the battlefield; their sympathy for her plight impresses their new master. Alban is appointed to rule Britain and continue the persecution. Amphiabel comes from Wales and converts him to Christianity. Maximinus arrests Alban, and Amphiabel flees back to Wales, pursued by the Roman army. Meanwhile, the Welsh Queen Winifred, also converted by Amphiabel, asks her suitor Sir Hugh for a moratorium on his wooing efforts; when he completes the agreed period, however, she announces that she has given herself wholly to heaven and cannot marry him. One of the Romans pursuing Amphiabel mocks the power of Winifred’s holy well, and smears the water on his eyes; it blinds him. His sight is restored, but he ascribes the miracle to the pagan gods. Winifred and Amphiabel are both arrested and brought to see the martyrdom of Alban. Leodice, Maximinus’ daughter, becomes enamoured of her new shoemaker, Crispin, who is really Offa; she continually finds reasons to asks for new shoes and new fitting sessions. She offers to show him the image of his future wife in a magic mirror; but in fact it is an ordinary mirror, and he sees her reflection. He confides that he is not really base-born, but a disguised prince, and they are secretly married. The Romans are mustering an army to resist an invasion of the Empire by Goths and Vandals. Eldred volunteers, and the shoemaker employs Sir Hugh to fill his place. Eldred distinguishes himself in battle: he saves Diocletian’s life, takes back the eagle standard after it has been seized by the enemy, and captures the Vandal king Roderick. The barbarians are expelled, and Diocletian knights Eldred. Sir Hugh is arrested after he sees Winifred a prisoner and reviles the Roman gods. Alban and Amphiabel are tortured in an attempt to make them renounce Christianity, but they remain steadfast and are sent for execution; Alban becomes Britain’s first Christian martyr ~ but not the last. Winifred is allowed to choose the manner of her own execution; she opts to bleed to death. Sir Hugh is made to drink her blood, mixed with poison.
