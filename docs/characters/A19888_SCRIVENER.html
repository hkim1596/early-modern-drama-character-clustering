Full speech

Faith never worse, Merchant's are wary, Tradesmen provident, Artificers turned frugal, and I think All the World will prove good husbands. Th'have been so bit already With taking up Commodities of brown paper, Buttons passed fashion, silks, and Satins, Babies, and Children's Fiddles, with Like trash Took up at a deer rate, and sold for trifles That now scarce one will bite. Faith sir Heaven knows, We Scriveners fare the worse by't. Yes, sir, I have one Spung that could you squeeze him, Would yield good substance. Is he well possessed? Yes sir, he hath in Acres glebe and meadow, Upland, and Dale, in woods and arable; And though in name a private Gentleman, Yet hath he three fair Lordship's, beside Sheep-walks, Parks, and other large Demesnes. I have dealt closely with a man of his To undermine him, one that sooths him up In all his riots, quarrels with his thrifty, Leads him to Game and guzzle in Vaulting houses, And places of bade fame. He's one to whom he trusts his whole Estate, And can do all things with him: In these Lands I promised him, if when they were in sail That I might bring his Chapman; a round sum, To which he seemed to listen. That's the man. Is it done? As fast as wax and witness can make good. Yes, and as great and use as ever you lent out money on. Redeem this did you say? tush, had he more, More he would soon send after; why he's all expense and riot. He spends all. He minds nor cares for nothing. Troth he's sinking, he's up to the neck already. Alas poor sheep, each Bramble shares his wool. Till he be fleeced quite. O these Dice, Drabs, and Drink? What will you do, sir? I am your Scrivener, and sir, I hope You'll not forget my pains? But sir, my money. I, but my Brokage? Yet let me have my endue. At your own charge? A Gallon sir, betwixt us two? Sir, I shall. Exeunt. Lord of this Soil; which I will never repurchase After his so base sullying. It is. Upon my— Upon my Ears it's true. Quindecem Michaelis, certainly the same. If you have the conscience to take the forfeit. You looked not in the Almanac For good and evil days. And pray what was the Motto. Alas poor man, all to no purpose, be better advised, Or it may be, kept out of his clutches; these, if we may Believe th'Astronomer, are ominous days. What then? Nihil in Bag? a scurvy critical Emblem, Presaging empty pockets, Why no Cutpurse Will trade when that's in power; then why would you Lend money in that unlucky hour? What's he that looks so ghastly? My Pen and Inkhorn bless me.

Plot summary

Plot — A New Trick to Cheat the Devil
Anne Changeable wants to marry Slightall, with her father’s consent, but her mother persuades her that marriage to Lord Scales will be better for her social standing. Slightall takes rejection badly: he turns to misogyny and voluptuous hedonism. One of his servants, Roger, tries unsuccessfully to dissuade him from squandering his money, while the other, Geoffrey, helps make an arrangement with a usurer to supply him with a further £1,000 — the usurer’s objective being to acquire his land. Lord Scales visits Anne, and she finds that he is not a good kisser: Slightall is the better catch in every respect save his lack of a title; but when she tries to reactivate the romance, Slightall rejects her. He has been ruined by prodigality and pays off his servants; Geoffrey is taken on by Lord Scales, but Roger declines a similar offer. Anne insults everyone who persuaded her to jilt Slightall, but this only makes Lord Scales more attracted to her. Changeable promises Anne that he will get Slightall back for her. Driven out of his wits with melancholy, Slightall conjures up Beelzebub, and makes a contract: the devil will give him enough money to pay his debts in exchange for the right to take his soul when he no longer owes anyone anything. On the day his debt to the usurer falls due, he turns out to have ample money to pay, so the usurer gets his money back rather than the lands he coveted. Asked by Anne to give Slightall some financial support, Lord Scales finds his offer rejected with contempt. The Changeables’ house is troubled by a female spirit; Slightall agrees to help exorcise it by spending the night in the haunted room. When he does so, he is visited by the ghost in Anne’s form. Beelzebub comes for Slightall’s soul, but Slightall demonstrates that he is not yet clear of debt: he paid the usurer with money loaned by Beelzebub. The devil accepts the situation and offers to marry him to the she-devil who haunts the Changeables’ house. Anne’s mother and Lord Scales give their consent, and watch as a friar performs the ceremony. In fact it is not a ghost in Anne’s form, but Anne herself; and ‘Beelzebub’ was really Changeable: Anne has finally got her man back. In the sub-plot, two friars are detained until late by charitable visitation, and find themselves locked out of their abbey. They ask Nan to give them a bed for the night.
