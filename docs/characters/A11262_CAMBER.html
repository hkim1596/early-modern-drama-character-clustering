My Lord. The grief of Niobe fair Athens queen, For her seven sons magnanimous in fi ld, For her seven daughters fairer then the fairest, Is not to be compared with my laments. And in the fields of martial Cambria, Close by the boisterous Iscans silver streams, Where lightfoot fairs skip from bank to bank, Full twenty thousand brave courageous knights Well exercised in feats of chivalry, In manly manner most invincible, Young Camber hath with gold and victual, All these and more, if needs shall more require, I offer up to venge my brother's death. The armestrong offspring of the doubted knight, Stout Hercules Alcmena's mighty son, That tamde the monsters of the threefold world, And rid the oppressed from the tyrants yokes, Did never show such valiantness in fight, As I will now for noble Albanact. So may we judge by her lamenting words. Oh Locrine hath she not a cause for to be sad? If she have cause to weep for Humbers death, And shed salt tears for her overthrow, Locrine may well bewail his proper grief, Locrine may move his own peculiar woe, He being conquered died a speedy death, And felt not long his lamentable smart, I being conqueror, live a lingering life, And feel the force of Cupid's sudden stroke. I gave him cause to die a speedy death, He left me cause to wish a speedy death. O that sweet face painted with nature's die, Those ros all checks mizt with a snowy white, That decent neck surpassing ivory, Those comely breasts which Venus well might spite, Are Like to snares which wily fowlers wrought Wherein my yielding heart is prisoner catch. The golden tresses of her dainty hair Which shine Like rubies glittering with the sun, Have so entrapped poor Locrines lovesick heart, That from the same no way it can be won. How true is that which oft I herd declared, One dram of joy, must have a pound of care. I uncle death is our common enemy, And none but death can match our matchless power Witness the fallen of Albioneus crew, Witness the fallen of Humber and his Huns, And this foul death hath now increased our woe, By taking Corineus from this life, And in his room leaving us world's of care. Pardon my brother noble Corineus, Pardon this once and it shall be amended.

Plot summary

Plot — Locrine, the Eldest Son of King Brutus
Brutus, the conqueror of Britain, is dying. He gives his eldest son Locrine the crown and betrothes him to Gwendoline, and assigns his other sons, Camber and Albanact, the south and north respectively. His funeral is swiftly followed by Locrine’s royal wedding. Strumbo is in love with Dorothy, and persuades her to marry him. However, he is impressed into Albanact’s army when the Scythians invade northern Britain, and Dorothy is killed when the Scythians burn his house with her inside. The Scythians win the battle, and Albanact takes his own life rather than submit. To avenge his death, Locrine, Camber, and the peers give battle and defeat the Scythians. Humber’s wife Estrild is captured by Locrine’s soldiers, and Locrine is attracted to her. Strumbo is caught having sex with Margery. Initially he refuses to marry her, but is forced to agree after she beats him up. He encounters the defeated and starving Humber, but the ghost of Albanact stops him from feeding the Scythian. Humber eventually drowns himself. Locrine is unable to put away Gwendoline and install Estrild as his queen because Gwendoline’s father, Corineus, proves inconveniently long-lived despite being wounded in the battle with the Scythians. Locrine hides her in an underground pleasure- chamber and visits her in secret; they have a child, Sabren. After Corineus finally dies, Gwendoline goes to Cornwall to arrange the funeral, and Locrine takes the opportunity to bring Estrild out of hiding. Gwendoline and her brother raise an army, and defeat Locrine in battle; he and Estrild kill themselves. Sabren finds their bodies but is physically too weak to use the sword on herself. Gwendoline finds her and promises her a lingering, unusual death, so Sabren drowns” herself; the river is named after her, Severn. Gwendoline arranges to bury Locrine as befits a king - and to leave Estrild ignominiously unburied.
