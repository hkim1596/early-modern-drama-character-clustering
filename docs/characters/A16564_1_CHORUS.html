Full speech

Sir, our estate some hasty help requires: In Misia near the celebarted rounds Of great Olympus which the world admires, There haunts a Boar the horror of these bounds: His body big, and hideous is his form, Whose foamy jaw with tusks Like javelins strikes, And in deformity all parts conform, His back hath bristles Like to iron pikes. This Nature's Monster, wondered at by men, The forests Tyrant, and the Countries terror, Does murder all, and draws them to his den, Who chance to cross his way by fatal error; In tears whilst melting, tender mother's wail, (The gored infants tumbling in their blood;) This beast to be abhorred does them assail, And in his bowels buries both for food; Then when we fly the field where he does haunt, To have his hunger, or his rage allayed, He all our labours quickly does supplant, And poor men's Hope's are strangely thus betrayed; Ere this, of true repose we were the types, And pasturing on each plain our fleecy flocks, Did make a consort of our warbling pipes With moving Crystals, playing on the rocks; And oft to ease our toils (all ranged in bans) With garlands guarded from Apollo's beams, We gazed upon Pactolus golden sands, Glassed, bathed, and quenched our thirst, with his pure streams; Whilst we preferred, the river seemed amazed, Even to his golden bed his grassy bank, And lay and looked whereas our cattle grazed, Far from all envy of a greater rank; That to repress oppression you take care, Though we were dumb, the public rest may speak: Your Laws, Like Spiders webs are not a snare For little flies, that them the big may break; Mean men by them from great men's pride are saved, The heavens continue long your prosperous reign, And let us not by such a beast be braved, Which by our ruin would your Sceptre stain. We crave none of your wealth, but wish to see This Boar be-bloud the staff of the most strong: Let valorous Atis worth ily your Son, With Lydian youth incapable of fears, Go to the fields before the rising Sun, To quench his thirst have drunk Sun, To quench his thirst have drunk the morning's tears, And we shall lead them crowned with laurel forth, Where in strict bounds, yet a theatre large For men to make a trial of their worth, They with advantage may this Monster charge; So shall we reap repose, and they delight, Whilst that prodigious body justly smarts, Though fearful once, then made a pleasant sighed, When Like a wood it planted is with darts. Oh how the king is moved at Atis death! His face the portrait of a passion bears, With bent eyes, crossed arms, and quivering breath, His Princely robe he desperately tears; Lo, with a silent pity-pleading look, Which shows with sorrow mixed a high disdain, He (while his soul seems to dissolve in smoke) Strays betwixt the corpses, and him who hath it slain. Lo, how he wounds himself despising pain, With leaden lights, weak legs, and head declined, The body beats the ground, as in disdain, That of her members one hath proved unkind; The fainting hand falls trembling from the Sword, With his self slaughtering blow for shame grown read, Which straight the blood pursues, with vengeance stored, To drown the same with the same floods it shed; Who of those parties can the combat show, Where both but one, one both struck and sustained? Or who triumphs for this most strange overthrow, Where as the victor lost, the vanquished gained? What heavy tidings hast thou now to tell, Who tearest thy garments thus? what forced thy flight? A hideous shout we herd the City give, Have foes prevailed, do they her beauty spoil? And is our Sovereign slain? He would say the reward. Then let our minds no more in doubt remain, And must we yield to that proud Strangers will? No doubt but high mishaps did then abound, Whilst with disdain the Conquerors bosom boiled, As some the sword, disgrace did some confound, Not only houses, Temples too were spoiled. Our joys are spoiled, his happiness expired, And no new chance can any comfort bring, Where destinies to ruin have conspired, Go woeful messenger, hold on thy course, For, to have herd too much, it irks our ears; And we shall note of this thy sad discourse, With sighs each accent, and each point with tears. Plot summary

Plot — Croesus
Solon visits Croesus, hoping to persuade him to give up his immoderate love of gold and think about less transitory things. He explains that all mortality is subject to change, and tells Croesus stories about the importance of posthumous fame, but Croesus considers them irrelevant to his situation as a king. Solon realizes that Croesus wili change only by losing the power which has made him arrogant. Adrastus is banished from Phrygia after accidentally killing his brother. (His intended victim was the man who poisoned his girlfriend.) Croesus has a nightmare about the death of his son Atys, and becomes excessively protective: he makes Atys marry in order to secure the succession, appoints him a bodyguard, and bans the type of sharp implement which caused his death in the dream. Mysia is ravaged by a wild boar, and its people appeal to Croesus for help. He is happy to hear that they don’t want his money, but distressed that they ask instead that he send Atys to hunt the boar. Atys protests at his father’s reluctance, so Croesus accedes, assigning Adrastus to look after him during the mission. Atys is killed, and Adrastus kills himself in remorse. In the past, Astyages, King of Media and Croesus’ brother-in-law, was perturbed by a prophecy that he would be deposed by his grandson. When the child was born, he tried to have it killed; but the herdsman who was given the job spared its life and adopted it as a replacement for his own stillborn baby. During his youth, Cyrus’ princely behaviour led to his identification. Astyages served Harpagus a banquet of his own son’s flesh, so Harpagus contacted Cyrus and raised a rebellion. Now Cyrus has led the Persian army to depose Astyages. Against his counsellor’s advice, Croesus plans to intervene and conquer Persia, but Cyrus anticipates him and attacks pre-emptively, ordering that Croesus be taken alive. Croesus is nearly killed by a Persian soldier, but saved when his dumb son cries out and identifies him. Cyrus intends to burn him at the stake, but is impressed by Croesus’ acknowledgement that Solon was right to warn him not to trust in worldly things. Cyrus frees him, and orders restraint in his soldiers’ looting.
