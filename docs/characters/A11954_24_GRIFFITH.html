How do's your Grace? Yes Madam: but I thank your Grace Out of the pain you suffered, gave no ear too't. Well, the voice goes Madam, For after the stout Earl Northumberland Arrested him at The ke, and brought him forward As a man sorely tainted, to his Answer, He fell sick suddenly, and grew so ill He could not fit his Mule. At last, with easy Rhodes, he came to Leicester, Lodged in the Abbey; where the reverend Abbot With all his Covent, honourably received him; To whom he gave these words. Oh Father Abbot, And old man, broken with the storms of State, Is come to lay his weary bones among you Give him a little earth for Charity. So went to bed; where eagerly his sickness Pursued him still, and three nights after this, About the hour of eight, which he himself Foretold should be his last, full of Repentance, Continual Meditations, Tears, and Sorrows, He gave his Honours to the world again, His blessed partly to Heaven, and slept in peace. Noble Madam: Men's evil manners, live in Brass, their Virtues We write in Water. May it please your Highness To hear me speak his good now? This Cardinal, Though from and humble Stock, undoubtedly Was fashioned to much Honour. From his Cradle He was a Scholar, and a ripe, and good one: Exceeding wise, fair spoken, and persuading: Lofty, and sour to them that loved him not: But, to those men that sought him, sweet as Summer. And though he were unsatisfied in getting, (Which was a sin) yet in bestowing, Madam, He was most Princely: Ever witness for him Those twins of Learning, that he raised in you, Ipswich and Oxford: one of which, fell with him, Unwilling to outlive the good that did it. The other (though unfinished) yet so Famous, So excellent in Art, and still so rising, That Christendom shall ever speak his Virtue. His Overthrow, heaped Happiness upon him: For then, and not till then, he felt himself, And found the Blessedness of being little And to add greater Honours to his Age Then man could give him; he died, fearing God. She is asleep: Good wench, let's fit down quiet, For fear we wake her. Softly, gentle Patience. Madam, we are here. None Madam. I am most joyful Madam, such good dreams Possess your Fancy. She is going Wench. Pray, pray. You are too blame, Knowing she will not lose her wonted Greatness To use so rude behaviour. Go too, kneel.

Plot summary

Plot — Henry the Eighth (All Is True)
The Kings of England and of France meet for a magnificent summit conference organized by Cardinal Wolsey. The English nobles resent Wolsey’s influence with the King, but Wolsey strikes first against the ringleader, Buckingham: evidence from his former surveyor, who bears him a grudge, secures his arrest; conviction for treason and execution follow. Queen Katherine persuades the King to repeal taxation which is causing hardship; Wolsey has it put about that his intercession secured this. King Henry attends a banquet at Wolsey’s house, performs in a masque of shepherds, and meets Anne Boleyn. Rumours circulate that he and the Queen are at odds: he claims to be troubled by the fact that she was originally married to his brother, but others notice that he is keen to marry Anne. Wolsey counsels divorce. A legatine court is summoned to judge the case, but Katherine refuses to co-operate and the court breaks up without reaching a decision. The King secretly marries Anne. The nobles conspire to overthrow Wolsey. Documents proving his corruption and treachery accidentally fall into the King’s hands, and Buckingham’s surviving allies serve him with articles of impeachment. He falls sick and dies. Anne is crowned Queen. Katherine too falls sick. Before her death she is visited by benevolent spirits, and by Caputius, the Imperial Ambassador, by whom she sends the King a request to love their daughter and provide for her former servants. Anne goes into labour and produces a daughter. A cabal of councillors attempts to overthrow Archbishop Cranmer, whom they consider a heretic. Before the hearing, Henry gives Cranmer his ring; the Archbishop produces it at a crucial moment, ensuring that the case is referred to the personal judgement of the King, who rebukes the conspirators. The vindicated Cranmer christens Henry’s new daughter, Elizabeth, and prophesies a golden future for England under her and her eventual successor.
