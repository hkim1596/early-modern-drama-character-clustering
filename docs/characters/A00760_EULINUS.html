The Court a wardrobe is of living shapes: And Ladies are the tissue-spangled suits, Which Nature wears on festival high days. The Court a Spring, each Madame is a Rose. The Court is Heaven, fair Ladies are the Stars. False Echo, don't blaspheme that glorious sex, Whose beauteous rays can strike rash gazers blind. Pray, leave this Cynic humour, while I sighs My Mistress praise: Her beauty's passed compare: Oh would she were more kind, or not so fair. Her modest smiles both curb and kindle love: The Court is dark without her; when She rises, The morning is her handmaid, strewing roses About loves Hemisphere: The lamps above Eclipse themselves for shame, To see her eyes Outshine their Chrysolites, and more bless the skies, Than they the Earth — Her body is a Crystal cage, whose pure Transparent mould not of gross elements Compacted, but the extracted Quintessence Of sweetest forms distilled: where Graces bright Do live immured, but not exempt from sighed. — Her model is beyond all Poets brains, And Painters pencells: All the lively Nymphs, Sire's, and Dryads, are but kitchenmaids, If you compare. To frame the Like Pandore, The Gods repine, and Nature would grow poor.— For here you find great Juno's stately front, Palla's grey eye, Venus her dimpled chin, Aurora's rosy fingers, the small wast Of Ceres daughter, and Medusa's hair, Before it hist:— Oh strange! what ignorant still? when as so plainly These Attributes describe her: why? She is A Rhapsody of Goddesses. The Elyxar Of all their several perfections. She is (Now bless your ears) by mortals called Landora. All steel and adamant. 'Tis beauty's pride, To stain Her lily white with blood of Lovers slain. Their groans make music, and their scalding sighs Raise a perfume, and vulturelike she gnaws Their bleeding hearts. No gifts, no learned flattery, No stratagems can work Landora's battery. As a tall Rock maintains majestic state, Though Boreas gallop on the tottering seas, And tilting split his froth out spurging waves Upon his surly breast: So she resists: And all my projects on her cruel heart, Are but retorted to their Authors smart. Most impious thoughts! Oh let me rather perish, And loving die, than living cease to love: And when I faint, let her but hear me cry, Aye me, there's none, which truly loves, but I. Your words are eare-awigges to my vexed brain Like henn-bane juice, or Aconite diffused They strike me senseless. My kinsman, and Hirildas to my end: But I'll near call you Counsellor, or Friend Adieu. Blessed be these means, and happy the Success. Plot summary

Plot — Fuimus Troes (The True Trojans)
The Romans have invaded Gaul. Caesar reaches the northern shore and thinks himself at the edge of the world, until he sees the white cliffs across the Channel: a new land to conquer. To justify a war, he sends Volusenus to make an offer of peace, expecting the Britons to refuse; Volusenus will also spy out the country in preparation for the Roman assault. News of the Roman invasion plans reaches King Cassibelan, who mobilizes his troops and allies. When Volusenus presents the terms, which require the Britons to pay tribute and send hostages, Cassibelan is advised to reject them, and does. Commius the Gaul makes a last effort to encourage him to come to terms, but is imprisoned for his pains. Caesar invades Britain, but sustains heavy casualties trying to get his troops ashore. Duke Nennius defeats Caesar in the battle, but is killed by Caesar’s poisoned sword. The celebration of the British victory is offset by Nennius’ state funeral. Cassibelan came to power by murdering a previous king and depriving his son Mandubrace of his throne. Seeking revenge, Mandubrace allies himself with Caesar, who agrees to launch a second attack on Britain. Against advice, Cassibelan decides to allow the Romans to land and face them in an inland battle. A storm wrecks forty of Caesar’s ships, and initially the Briton army has the upper hand. The tide turns after many of the British petty kingdoms switch their allegiance to Mandubrace, and Cassibelan realizes that the island will be conquered. The Romans capture a druid, who warns Caesar to concentrate on foreign wars, avoid the Senate House, and beware of Brutus, who will avenge his treatment of the British descendants of Trojan Brute. Using Commius as an intermediary, Cassibelan sues for peace. Caesar dictates terms: Mandubrace is to be restored to his birthright, Cassibelan to pay tribute to Rome, and a tower to be built in Troynovant as a monument to the conquest. With Britain part of the Roman empire, a time of universal peace comes to the world. In the sub-plot, Eulinus loves Landora, who in turn loves his kinsman Hirildas and invites him into her bed. Hirildas bribes her servant Rollano to admit Eulinus instead. At Nennius’ funeral, the two friends fall out during a bout of sword-play and Eulinus kills Hirildas. This leaves him with a problem: he will not now be able to go to bed with Landora posing as Hirildas.
