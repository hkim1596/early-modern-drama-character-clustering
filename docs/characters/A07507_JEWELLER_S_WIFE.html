Is my sweet Knight coming, are you cer taine he's coming? A Barbers shop, Oh he's a trim Knight, would he venture his body into a Barbers shop, when he knows 'tis as dangerous as a piece of Ireland: Oh yonder, yonder, he comes, get you back again, and look you say as I aduiz'de you. My mask, my mask. My pleasure welcome: I have God single, none but you shall accompany me to the Justice of peace my Father's. Weeping cozen? News as cold to the heart as and old men kind ness: my uncle dead? No Gentleman sir, he's a Knight. He's my husbands own brother, I can tell you (sir: I can assure you father, my husband and he has lain both in one belly. I told you his affection. But here I bar you from all plots to night, The time is yet too heavy to be light. Well we shall see your temper. I'll do my best endeavour to multiply, I assure you. No by my faith.— And do but whirl the Ring a'th door once a bout, my maidservant shall be taught to understand the language. Good rest my effectual pleasure. Where art thou, Oh my Knight! I say you're my knight, for I'm sure I paid for you Now out upon the Marmoset, hast thou served me so long, and offer to bring in a Candle? How happened it you came so soon, I looked not for you these two hours: yet as the sweet chance is, you came as well as a thing could come, for my hus: band's newly brought a bed. He never sent him any thing since I knew him, he's a man of a bade nature to his wife, none but his Maids can thrive under him. I, judge whither I have a cause to be a Courtesan or no? to do as I do, and elderly fellow as he is, if he were married to a young Virgin, he were able to break her heart, though he could break nothing else: here, here, there's joust a hundred and fifty, but I stole e'm so hardly from him, twold ee'ne have grieved you to have seen it. Therefore prithee my sweet pleasure, do not keep company so much, how do you think I am a ble to maintain you? though I be a Jewellers wife, Jewels are Like women, they rise & fall, we must be con tent to lose sometime, to gain often, but you're content always to lose, and never to gain: what needs you ride with a Footman before you? No, I would not have you so base neither: but now and then, when you keep your Chamber, you might let your Footman out for eighteen pence a day, a great relief at years end I can tell you. Nay, I've a greater quarrel to you yet. Nay more, you swore to me, that you were the first that taught him to ride a great horse, and tread the Ring with agility. O take heed, you cannot have that, 'tis always in the eye of my husband. Give me thy hand, isle lead thee forth. Who would not love a friend at Court? what fine Galleries and rooms am I brought through? I had thought my knight durst not have shown his face here, I. Ah me! This is the worst room that ever I came in. Your worship is to great for me to know: I'm but a small timbred-woman when I'm out of my apparel, and dare not venture upon greatness. That purse? Oh death has the Knight served me so? given away my Favours? 'Tis long of those, an't Like your grace, that come in upon us, & will never leave marrying of our widows till they make they all as free as their first husbands. Let me have pardon I beseech your grace, and I'll peach they all, all the close women that are, and upon my knowledge there's above five thousand within the walls and the liberties. I will hereafter live so modestly, I will not lie with mine own husband, nor come near a man in the way of honesty. My father! 'tis mine own father. My father! i'faith sir I, simply though I stand here a Citizens wife: I am a Justice of Peaces Daughter. Why then let him run a little before I beseech thee, for a my troth he will discover us else. The grace? 'tis sufficient grace that y'aue a Horse to ride upon, you should think thus with your self e very time you go to bed if my head were laid, what would become of that horse? he would run a bade raze then as well as his Master. You made me believe at first the Prince had you in great estimation, and would not offer to Travail without you, nay, that he could not travail without your Direction and intelligence. Why should I desire your company else? What and idle question's that? why do you hinke I cannot make room for you in my Father's house, as well as in my husbands, th'are both good for nothing else? Nay Father though I say it, that should not say it, he looks to me more Like a husband then a Kins man. Lady, that word is worth and hundred Angels at all times, for it cost more: if I live till tomorrow night, my sweet pleasure thou shalt have them. Come ee'n much about this time, when Ta uerns give up their Ghosts, and Gentlemen are in their first cast.— No, if it were as much more, I would think it foul scorn to chide you, I advise you to be thrifty, to take the time now, while you have it, you shall seldom get such and other fool as I am, I warrant you: why there's Mettrezza Auriola keeps her love with half the cost that I am at, her friend can go a foot Like a good Husband, walk in wosted Stockings, and inquire for the Sixpenny ordinary. O are you true? Why lo, who would love you now but a Citti zens wife? so inconstant, so for sworn: you say women are false Creatures, but take away men, and thei'd be honester then you—Nay last of all, which offends me most of all, you told me you could countenance me at Court, and you know we esteem a friend there, more worth then a Husband here. That's your word indeed, and upon that con dition take it this kiss and my love for ever. You maintain? you seen it court?

Plot summary

Plot — The Phoenix
The old Duke of Ferrara has reigned 45 years, and is now near death. He accepts his nobles’ advice that his son, ===== PAGE 76 ===== Prince Phoenix, should travel to other nations in order to prepare himself for government. Phoenix suspects a stratagem to have him out of the country at the time his father dies, jeopardizing the succession, so he decides instead to travel incognito within Ferrara itself, witnessing abuses which require reformation, but which would be concealed from a visitor known to be of high rank; he will be attended only by his servant Fidelio. Fidelio has a new stepfather, a sea-captain: against all advice, his mother Castiza has remarried, only to find herself tied to an abusive bully. The captain also regrets the marriage and is looking for a way out of it; so he agrees to sell his wife to the courtier Proditor. Phoenix and Fidelio hear of this and infiltrate the sales meeting in disguise: Fidelio poses as the scrivener who has.drawn up the contract, and Phoenix as a potential investor in the captain’s next voyage. Once the contract is signed and the money handed over, they confront the captain and force him to go into exile, giving the money to Castiza. Fidelio’s girlfriend has just lost her father, whose will leaves her in the custody of her uncle, the corrupt Justice Falso. He proposes to marry her, threatening to withhold payment of her dowry if she should marry anyone else (such as Fidelio). Fidelio goes to see her in disguise, bringing a letter and gift from himself, but on the way he and Phoenix are attacked by robbers and the token is stolen. The thieves are in fact Falso’s servants in disguise, seeking to supplement the meagre wage he pays them. Phoenix captures one of them and has him brought before a judge; but this happens to be Falso himself, and he commits the prisoner to the custody of the other servants, his accomplices. The niece explains to Phoenix what has happened and absconds with him and Fidelio. Falso also abets his own daughter’s adultery with her husband’s brother, a knight. She sets up an assignation, promising to give him money: he is gently to disturb the door-knocker after her jeweller husband has gone to bed, whereupon the maid will let him in. Phoenix accidentally gives the signal and is admitted by mistake. Meeting him in darkness, the wife does not notice the error, and gives him the money. Meanwhile the knight is arrested for debt.
