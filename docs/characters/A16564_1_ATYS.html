Full speech

Ah! wherein Father did I thus offend? Or what vile sign of a degener'd mind Have you but marked in me, whose course may tend To the reproach of our imperial kind? And abject dastard, who for nought avails, Whose worth the world must trust, but never try As one whose strength, or then his Courage fails Must I in vile repose inglorious lie! Lie Like a wanton by vain thoughts bewicth'd! Who spoiled of force, effeminately lives, A Peacok poor, with painted pens enriched Yet bore of every thing that glory gives; What glory give those titles unto me, Which by succession fallen, not by desert? Should but my fame with borrowed feathers fly? For, come of kings a kingdom is my partly; "Who honour as hereditary claims, "Like bastards base, does but his birthright bloat, I scorn to beg my worth from dead men's names, Or to gain credit only by my Coat; What comfort's this to have the highest seat, And all the bliss that Majesty imparts, If those whom only we exceed in State, Be our Superiors in far better parts? "More then a Crown true worth should be esteemed "Th'one Fortune's gift, the other is our own, "By which the mind from anguish is redeemed, When Fortune's goods are by her self overthrown. Would God I had some means once ere my death, To satisfy that infinite desert, Which I shall hold, so long as I have breath, Deep registered with reverence in my heart; "Yet (sir) we see this is a natural thing, "That too excessive love engenders fears: A sport Like this can no great peril bring, Where either all delights the eyes, or ears. If from my former deeds I now should shrink, (As void of virtue) to soft pleasure thrall, Of your two Sons what might your Subject's think, Th'one wanting but one sense, the other all? What fancies might my late spoused love possess, To see her husband hateful in men's sights, And honours bounds thus basely to transgress, As womanized still wallowing in delights? "Though women would have men at their devotion, "They hate base minds that hatch no noble notion. Now bent to see this monsters ugly shape, With and inflamed desire my thought do burn, And Father fear not, dream of no mishap, I hope with speed victorious to return. Life of my soul, how do such broken speeches, From troubled passion thus abruptly rise? I know (my love) thy love my mind o're-reaches: "Affection (Schooled with fears) is too too wise; I go alongst the fields, for sport to range; Thy sighs do but my soul with sorrow fill; And pardon (deer) I find this wondrous strange, That thou beginnest now to resist my will; If I trespass in ought against my duty, Which makes thee thus my constancy mistrust; Mistrust not yet the Chains of thine own beauty, Which bind all my desires, and so they must; Are we not now made one? such fears overcome, Though I would fly, my self my self do fetter, And if that I would fly, from whom? to whom? I can love none so well, none loves me better; Have pity of those pearls, (sweet eyes, souls pleasures) Least they presage what thou wouldst not have done; The Heavens had not give me those precious treasures, Of such perfections to be spoiled so soon.

Plot summary

Plot — Croesus
Solon visits Croesus, hoping to persuade him to give up his immoderate love of gold and think about less transitory things. He explains that all mortality is subject to change, and tells Croesus stories about the importance of posthumous fame, but Croesus considers them irrelevant to his situation as a king. Solon realizes that Croesus wili change only by losing the power which has made him arrogant. Adrastus is banished from Phrygia after accidentally killing his brother. (His intended victim was the man who poisoned his girlfriend.) Croesus has a nightmare about the death of his son Atys, and becomes excessively protective: he makes Atys marry in order to secure the succession, appoints him a bodyguard, and bans the type of sharp implement which caused his death in the dream. Mysia is ravaged by a wild boar, and its people appeal to Croesus for help. He is happy to hear that they don’t want his money, but distressed that they ask instead that he send Atys to hunt the boar. Atys protests at his father’s reluctance, so Croesus accedes, assigning Adrastus to look after him during the mission. Atys is killed, and Adrastus kills himself in remorse. In the past, Astyages, King of Media and Croesus’ brother-in-law, was perturbed by a prophecy that he would be deposed by his grandson. When the child was born, he tried to have it killed; but the herdsman who was given the job spared its life and adopted it as a replacement for his own stillborn baby. During his youth, Cyrus’ princely behaviour led to his identification. Astyages served Harpagus a banquet of his own son’s flesh, so Harpagus contacted Cyrus and raised a rebellion. Now Cyrus has led the Persian army to depose Astyages. Against his counsellor’s advice, Croesus plans to intervene and conquer Persia, but Cyrus anticipates him and attacks pre-emptively, ordering that Croesus be taken alive. Croesus is nearly killed by a Persian soldier, but saved when his dumb son cries out and identifies him. Cyrus intends to burn him at the stake, but is impressed by Croesus’ acknowledgement that Solon was right to warn him not to trust in worldly things. Cyrus frees him, and orders restraint in his soldiers’ looting.
