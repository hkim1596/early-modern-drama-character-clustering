The noble King and Queen of Gallia first salutes, By me, their honourable father, my Lord Leir: Next, they commend them kindly to your Graces, As those whose welfare they entirely wish, Letters I have to deliver to my Lord Leir, And presents too, if I might speak with him. Pardon me, Madam; for I think not so, But say so only, cause he is not here. I had incharge to do it to himself. Exceeding well, and never sick one hour, Since first she set her foot upon the shore. I hope, not so, Madam. O no, her grief exceeds, until the time, That she be reconciled unto her father. What, Madam? Amen to that: but God release her grief, And send her father in a better mind, Then to continue always so unkind. Madam, I hope your Grace will do the Like. To expiate or mitigate his wrath: For he hath misconceived without a cause. 'Tis pity it should be so, would it were other wise. Then how, Madam? It shows you bear and honourable mind. There is of late news come unto the Court, That old Lord Leir remains in Cambria: I'll hie me thither presently, to impart My letters and my message unto him. I never was less welcome to a place In all my life time, then I have been hither, Especially unto the stately Queen, Who would not cast one gracious look on me, But still with lowering and suspicious eyes, Would take exceptions at each word I spoke, And fain she would have undermined me, To know what my Ambassage did import: But she is Like to hop without her hope, And in this matter for to want her will, Though (by report) sheele hau't in all things else. Well, I will post away for Cambria: Within these few days I hope to be there, All happiness unto the Cambrian King. I came from Gallia, unto Cornwall sent, With letters to your honourable father, Whom there not finding, as I did expect, I was directed hither to repair. My letters, Madam, will import the same, Which my Commission is for to deliver. I must perform my charge in such a manner, As I have strict commandment from the King. Madam, I hope, not far off. Madam, your threats no whit appall my mind, I know my conscience guiltless of this act; My King and Queen, I dare be sworn, are free From any thought of such impiety: And therefore, Madam, you have done them wrong, And ill beseeming with a sisters love, Who in mere duty tender him as much, As ever you respected him for dowry. The King your husband will not say as much. Pray God some near you have not done the Like. Never was I offered such discourtesy; God and my King, I trust, ere it be long, Will find a mean to remedy this wrong, No, Madam, I said quite contrary.

Plot summary

Plot — The True Chronicle of King Leir (King Lear and his Three Daughters)
King Lear deems it a good moment to marry off his three daughters: with his wife recently dead, they no longer have a mother to direct them. His plan is to retire and divide the kingdom between them as their dowries, and to marry them to neighbouring kings in order to forestall foreign aggression. Cordella, however, is determined to marry for love rather than for diplomatic convenience, so Lear plans to circumvent her by laying on a test: the daughters will make a public declaration of how much they love their father, and he will then ask Cordella to show that love by complying with his wishes over her marriage. Scaliger warns the other daughters, Goneril and Regan, of what Lear has in mind, and misleads them into believing that their shares of the kingdom will reflect the extent of their declared love. Jealous of their younger sister’s beauty and renown, they flatter Lear so that Cordella’s simple statement of due filial love pales in comparison. Lear gives the kingdom wholly to them, summons the Kings of Cornwall and Cambria to be their husbands, and assigns them their shares by lot. Against the advice of the good counsellor Perillus, Lear banishes Cordella from court, and she plans to make her living working as a seamstress. By chance, she meets the King of France, who has come to Britain disguised in search of a wife, and he takes her back to France as his Queen. Lear goes to live with Cornwall and Goneril, but annoys her with his criticisms of her extravagance. Scaliger advises her that halving his allowance will persuade him to take himself off to Regan, and so indeed it does. Cornwall sends a messenger to Regan asking for news of Lear, but Goneril bribes him to deliver instead a libellous letter about her father’s behaviour; she also encourages him to murder Lear. Regan has the same idea, and sets up an opportunity, but at the last moment the messenger is struck by conscience, admits the truth to his intended victim: and runs away.
