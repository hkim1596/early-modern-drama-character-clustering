I will my Lord not once partly from your side, take you your rest, your travels do it crave, Here fast by you I am resolved to bide, to guard you so, as nought your rest deprave. The grief of mind I see works wondrous things, commanding all estates both Lords and kings. To find his daughter out these pains he took. For great offence my Lord the payment great, the meanest man feels not the greatest fallen, You rew with time that you did work in heat, and yet you find to comfort you withal: This cost to Sibilles words so does agree. But sir behold what Ladies do I see? A royal blood her virtue will bew ray, though Fortune seek her near so to oppress, And noble raze will not run far astray. but of her self will work her own redress: As I my self even now have found most true, in this your Ladies case whom I so rue. She fearing father's wrath for her offence, though by constraint unkindly caused to stray, As she intends with speed departure hence, so will she not but wisely partly away, And for advise resorted unto me, to learn what way her best return might be. My Council was, since fates had found the mean, the English Queen to make for her defence, To whose assured stay she might welleane to suage her father's wrath, so wrought for her offence: For none could help her more nor so as she, if with such suit her grace content might be. Her credit is so good her fame so flies, her Honour such, her wisdom so in note, Her name so known to all men's ears and eyes, as better mean could no where else be got, Then if he might at her hands understand, what she hath herd and seen within her land. Whereon when we resolved by joint assent, and I at her request was drawing near, To move the suit according as we meant, I met you by the way which had been here: By whom I learned a very speedy mean, to work her weal and void all terror clean. But maid where is the Duke of whom you spoke, which took this toil for your good Ladies sake? It will be hard her settled love to shake, which grounded once is not light to remove, Yet for your love and for her Countries sake, it may fallen out she will forget her love: Which being new and young did ravish so, now being old hath better leave to go, But yonder comes the maiden which was sent, and lo my Lady there for whom she went. Fair Lady this your courteous speech does crave, disclose of all that careful breast does hide, In him that lies here the world may have, wherein with maze to let their minds abide. A Prince he is, whom fortune does constrain, with fruitless toil to travel still in vain. And Asian Lord the great Cambaian Duke. Because in love her mind he did displease. Even so it was: for he from court removed her friend, for whom her country she forsook, As not of force her Lovers lack to bear: which known, the Duke to travel him betook: To find her out whom Nature made so deer, With mind resolved if he her met again, to think such hap sweet pay for all his pain. Not yet, but that Sibilla bade him go, to such a soil as I suppose is this, and there to have his hope and end his woe, Gaudina she is called of worthy fame. I thank you for your courtesy. My Lord, if words that pass from faithful heart may stay your mind, my hope here bids me stay, For marking all that's here in every partly, and minding that which Sibil once did say: Me think this place should be the happy land, where we should rest, as she bore us in hand. Beside while you took rest, a Lady came with show of grief, that your mishaps were such, And learning both yours and your daughters name, did pass away: all which persuade me much: That if you stay till she again return, your heavy heart with joyful news shall burn.

Plot — The Queen's Entertainment at Woodstock
[The start of the entertainment is lost. The knights Loricus and Contarenus fight on horseback, watched by Lady Gaudina. They are interrupted by Hemetes the hermit.] He conducts them to the Queen’s bower, where he recounts the various characters’ histories. Less than seven years earlier, in the land of Cambia, Gaudina’s father objected to her love for Contarenus because of his low rank, and paid an enchantress to ===== PAGE 140 ===== send the knight to another country by magic. Learning of his actions, Gaudina left Cambia and met Loricus, who also left his own country after a disappointment in love; since then they have travelled together. Hemetes himself, meanwhile, was a knight blinded at the temple of Venus for the division of allegiance between love and learning, but compensated by Apollo with the gift of prophecy in matters of love. Three separate predictions by the unnamed enchantress, Sibylla, and Apollo mean that all four characters have anticipated achieving satisfaction (and, in Hemetes’ case, the restoration of his sight) in circumstances which have just come to pass: in an ideal state, England, two worthy knights have fought, two lovers met, and the events have been watched by the worthiest lady in the world, Queen Elizabeth. Hemetes asks the Queen to entertain Gaudina and Contarenus at court pending their return home to Cambia. The Queen accompanies the hermit back to his house, where she is served a banquet. The fairy queen pays a visit, and the Queen and her maids of honour receive gifts. Afterwards the Queen returns home in her coach and hears a song from inside an oak. Wanting to make her peace with her father, Gaudina consults the fairy queen for advice on the readiest way to Cambia. Occanon, however, is no longer there: having wandered the world in search of his lost daughter, he is now in England. Their reunion is marked by a resumption of their disagreement about her love-life: he urges her to give up Contarenus for the good of her country, but she holds the line that her lover’s virtue makes up for any deficiency in his rank. Occanon has more success persuading Contarenus to withdraw, but the knight refuses his compensatory offer of a noble life in Cambia: he prefers not to suffer the sight of his love with another man, but would rather join with Loricus in lamenting the crosses of fortune.
