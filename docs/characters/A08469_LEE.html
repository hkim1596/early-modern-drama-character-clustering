A murder closely done and in my ground? Search carefully, if any where it were, This obscure thicket is the likeliest place. This bloody towel, and these naked knives, Beside we found them sitting by the place, Where the dead body lay within a bush. And for amends, Touching the wrong unwittingly I have done, There are a few crowns more for them to drink. gives them a purse. Look if thou knowest him, turn his body up, A lack it is my son, my son and heir, Whom two years since, I sent to Ireland, To practice there the discipline of war, And coming home (for so he wrote to me) Some savage heart, some bloody devilish hand, Either in hate, or thirsting for his coin, Hath here slucde out his blood, unhappy hour, Accursed place, but most inconstant fate, That hadst reserude him from the bullets fire, And suffered him to escape the wood-karns fury, Didst here ordain the treasure of his life, (Even here within the arms of tender peace, And where security gate greatest hope) To be consumed by treasons wasteful hand? And what is most afflicting to my soul, That this his death and murder should be wrought, With hout the nowledge by whose means it done, Just judgement of that power, whose gracious eye, Loathing the sighed of such a heinous fact, Dazzled their senses with benumming-sleepe, Till their unhallowed treachery were known: Awake you monsters, murderers awake, Tremble for horror, blush you cannot choose, Beholding this inhumane deed of yours. O devilish! can you boast unto your selves Of quiet sleep, having within your hearts The gilded of murder waking, that with cries Deafs the loud thunder, and solicits heaven, With more e than Mandrakes shrieks for your offence? Can you deny the fact? see you not here, The body of my son by you misdone? Look on his wounds, look on his purple hew: Do we not find you where the deed was done? Were not your knives fast closed in your hands? Is not this cloth and argument beside, Thus stained and spotted with his innocent blood? These speaking characters, were nothing else To plead against you, would convict you both. Bring them away, bereavers of my joy, At Hartford where the Sises now are kept, Their lives shall answer for my sons lost life. As I am wronged, so may the law proceed. exeunt. Is this the wolf whose thirsty throat did drink My deer sons blood? art thou the snake He cherished, yet with envious piercing sting, Assaildst him mortally? foul stigmatike, Thou venom of the country where thou livedst, And pestilence of this: were it not that law Stands ready to revenge thy cruelty, Traitor to God, thy master, and to me, These hands should be thy executioner.

Plot summary

Plot — 1 Sir John Oldcastle
Despite a royal prohibition on disorder during the preparations for the invasion of France, Lords Herbert and Po·wys fall out over their religious differences, and brawl with their followers in the streets of Hereford;Herbert is wounded, and Powys flees, with orders given for his arrest. The Bishop of Rochester does his best to associate dissent and conspiracy with Sir John Oldcastle, but the King is not convinced. Powys takes refuge in Oldcastle's house, just before two orders arrive: one to attend at court, the other to answer charges before Rochester's ecclesiastical court; Oldcastle's servant Harpoole forces Rochester's summoner to eat his own summons, wax and all. The King advises Oldcastle to keep his religious views private: he can believe what he likes so long as he does not antagonize the bishops or encourage Protestant rebellion. In turn. Oldcastle intercedes for Powys, and the King signs his pardon. Rebellion is brewing in England. On the basis of a superior dynastic claim, the Earl of Cambridge intends to seize the throne with French support. Oldcastle is invited to join the plot, but he procures an incriminating document and takes it to court. Meanwhile, Protestant rebels muster their forces in London. The King plans to spy on them in disguise, but is robbed by the renegade priest Sir John; the priest gets him to promise to secure the King's pardon for robbery, and gives him half a coin as a token by which they can know one another again. On the night before the battle, the King. in his own person, plays dice with Sir John and wins back his stolen money, including the broken coin; as promised, he agrees to spare Sir John's life ifhe gives up his life of crime. The rebels are routed, but the prisoners falsely implicate Oldcastle. The charges collapse when it becomes clear that the rebels don't recognize him, and Oldcastle further demonstrates his loyalty with his evidence of the Cambridge conspiracy. The rebel leaders are executed, Cambridge exposed and arrested, and the King's faith in Oldcastle is restored. In the King's absence, Rochester has Oldcastle's property seized, and Oldcastle himself arrested for treason when he arrives home; his books are burnt when they are found to be in English. Rochester visits Oldcastle in prison in the hope that he may recant, but Oldcastle overpowers him and escapes in his clothes. On the run, he arrives with his wife in St Albans, but is forced to escape arrest by another illicit exchange of clothes.…
