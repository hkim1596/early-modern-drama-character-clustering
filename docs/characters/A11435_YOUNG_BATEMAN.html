My sweetest Nan? Pray thee no more? Tears are the Heralds to future sorrows, I have collected all that's man together And wrastld with affections as with streams, And as they strive that do oppress the billows So do I fare in each external partly. My Acts are Like the motionall gymmalls Fixed in a Watch, who wind themselves away Without cessation; here if I stay, I find I must be where thou art! which when I am Thy father's rage increases Like a flame Fed by ungentle blasts! my absence May work those bitter sweeteings from his heart And smooth the rising furrows in his brow. It is sufficient that I know thee firm Fixed as a Rock in constancy, and love, Able to shipwreck greatness, and despises A violated heart, as a disease. I go to Leith as children go to school Studying what shall please my Mistress best, My lesson cond, I will return again And dedicate my labours unto thee. Alas what wilt thou do. Is virtue grown to so absurd a rate It gains no better credit with base worldlings. Dear sir spare your fury? Anger in old men is a Lunacy That wounds the speakers, not the spectators? My thoughts are now embarked to go for Leith And see the Wars, I hope ever my return I shall find temperate weather in your looks, And all these storms vanishd. With my life sir. Prithee leave us. Now Nan heres none but thou, and I; thy love Emboldens me to speak, and cheerfully Here is a piece of gold, 'tis but a little one Yet big enough to ty, and seal a knot A jugal knot on Earth, to which high heaven Now cries Amen, say thou so too, and then When either of us breaks this sacred bound Let us be made strange spectacles to the world To heaven, and earth. Thou now art Like a pollishd ivory Table In pureness without; or stain or blemish. If thou shouldst soil this whiteness with black deeds Think what a monster thou wouldst make thy self. I doubt thee not, but give this cautionary. Hark the Drum beats, from the arms of love I now must burnish in the Arms of war, adue. Whose Colours I display. I thank your honour. Sir have you dispatched me? I construe it? That your suspicion deems it to be love, In sooth it needs not, such a constant rock My love is built on that it cannot fallen. I cannot fasten jealousy in my thoughts, Knowing her loyalty; great excuses For my intended journey know I none: And to frame any were but negatives? Yet in my sleeps I have strange visions, Which waking I cannot thrust from memory. I do beseech your licence, let me go. I will be mindful of you all farewell. I wear that visage formerly I did, Six Moons has not so metamorphosed me, But that I may be known? all my friends; My familiar sociates, and acquaintance Carelessly pass me with a heavy glance As if I were some rioter, or prodigal Who having ship wracked reputation After and act of bankrupt, compounds With debtor, and creditor; others Shake me by'th hand, but with such lenity As if I burned them? or that I from the wars Had brought home some diseases, as Killing As the Plague, or more infectious. My father whither for joy or sorrow, As tears be answerable to both passion, But he wept, cried welcome home, and sighed, As if some drops of blood fell from his heart Heaven has a hand in all things; if that My Nan be well, we will dispense with griefs, Of lower kinds Kind, cozen Ursula. How fares my sweetest Nan? The meaning of this Music? A Wedding, and here this day. Bless me what prodigious Object Is yond, that blasts mine eyes, and Like a thief, Steals my understanding! certes 'tis she Is it not speak Ursula? Now by my life. Strange fears assail my senses, and begins Conflicts of despairs, doubts, and fears, And but I have a resolution fixed On her fidelity; this frontispice And other entertainments might confirm Former presages. Her sighed Like to a cordial has expelled All former gross suggestions, me thinks I taste my happiness ever I touch it. She's dumb with joy, and I Like to a man Entranced with joys unutterable, cannot speak? But I have lost my self, I am awake, And see a substance more then dreamers do, Thus in the arms of love I do enfold thee. Alive or dead I must, and will enjoy thee, It was my promise? I cannot choose but weep. I have not cursed thee yet, remember that. It 'tis resolved! life is too burdensome, I've born while I can, and have suppressed All insurrections pale Death has made. It is my terror that I live to think I bear a life that is offensive to me. Pale monster in thy meagerest aspect Come, and affront me; fill thy paunched nerves With my hearts blood; till with the overture Thy never satisfied maw be sated? But cowardly monster thou approchest none But those that fly thee, and Like to greatness Wouldst be so elivated for doing good, That of thy self thou never didst intend. Poor Snakes that are in worldly sorrows sowrst Cannot participate thy Ebon Dart. 'Tis said thou art not partial, and dost wind The Prince, the beggar, and the potentate All in one mould; but they do falsify That say thou art so tiranously just, For I have sought thee through the pend groves, The shady cells where melancholy walks, And echo-like thou answerst me with Death, But darest not show thy face; the world's monarch In three fits of and Ague died. Some flies, Some silly gnats can kill! let me consume then mayest thou brag thy conquest, that thou slewest What neither love nor hatred could destroy. Since thou disdainest me, I disdain thy power, There be a thousand ways to cozen Death Behold a Tree, just at her door a fruitless Tree That has in autumn cast her leavy boughs Sorry to show such fruit as she produces. The night seems silent, sleep charms the house, And now the perjured woman is a topping, I'll climb as high as she, yet i'll not rest, My airy ghost shall find her where she lies, And to her face divulge her perjuries. Night be auspicious, draw thy sable weeds, For daylight is a ashamed of her black deeds One twich will do't, and then I shall be wed As firm unto my grave, as to her bed. Swear not sweet Nan! The book of fate, as now may be unclasped And record what thou speakest. Thy constancy I resalute. Death only separates me from thy love. Alive or dead I shall enjoy thee then Spite of thy father's frowns. Thou art merry still. I wonder then how I dare know my self, When thou forgettest me? I had thought Had I been sullide with the sooty More, Or tan'd with heat Like some Egyptian slave, Or spoted Like the Persian Leopards, Or in the worst form can be termn'd, Or imagined, yet thou coulds have known me, I am thy Bateman Nan! Strengthen me you ever Hallowed Powers, Guard me with patience that I may not curse, Be see I loved her; be assured this, Alive or dead thy promise thou shall keep I must, and will enjoy thee? Ah monstrous; she plays with my disasters As boys with bubbles blown up into air, You that have care of innocents be my guard Least I commit some outrage on my self. For such and overture, and flood of woes Surroundes me; that they almost drowned My understanding; thy perjuries shall be writ With pens of Diamonds upon Leaves of steel, And kept as statutes are to show the world. Plot summary

Plot — The Vow Breaker, or The Fair Maid of Clifton
Though Young Bateman is betrothed to Anne Boot and loved by her, her father reneges on the ===== PAGE 347 ===== 1628 arrangement. Meanwhile, war looms: England enters an alliance with a federation of Scottish lords hostile to the French Queen Regent of Scotland, and troops are being recruited to march north. Bateman joins up, hoping that his absence will make Old Boot’s heart grow fonder, and Anne vows to love only him; but her father puts pressure on her to marry German, and, seduced by his wealth, she agrees. The French are vanquished, and Bateman distinguishes himself in battle; but he is troubled by dreams about Anne, and returns home on the day of her wedding, just missing the ceremony. German goes away on a long business trip, leaving Anne in her father’s house. Old Bateman sues Boot for breach of the pre-contract; Young Bateman hangs himself outside Anne’s door, and his ghost haunts Anne, invisible to the rest of her family. Anne gives birth to a baby girl. While the women around her sleep, she sees the ghost and runs out of the house; following her footsteps in the snow, the household find her drowned in the river. The two bereaved fathers, Boot and Bateman, commiserate together. In Scotland, Sir Gervase Clifton insults the Queen Regent by drawing an unfavourable comparison with Queen Elizabeth, and captures Martigues in a single combat, but releases him with honour. Joshua the Puritan intends to hang his cat for Sabbath-breaking - she killed a mouse on Sunday - but the cat is saved by the arrival of a French ambassador. The English attack Leith, and Clifton captures a French colonel’s armour. Hostilities are ended by diplomatic means, and Scotland is to be demilitarized. The English and French lords feast together, and Joshua gets drunk. The English troops return south to meet the Queen at Nottingham; the common soldiers plan to put on a play for her, but decide on a morris dance instead. She meets the civic authorities and rewards her military commanders.
