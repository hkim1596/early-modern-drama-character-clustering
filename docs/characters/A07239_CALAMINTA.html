It's of itself Curious and rare: but borrowing ornament As it does from your Grace, that dame sto wear it, Incomparable. I cannot, Your Excellence is above it. With much reverence, I dare not say affection. He expressed More ceremony in his humble thanks Then feeling of the favour; and appeared Wilfully ignorant in my opinion Of what it did invite him to. Not any, and this does amaze me Madame, That he, a Soldier, one that drinks rich wines, Feeds high, and promises as much as Venus Could wish to find from Mars, should in his manners Be so averse to women. And that way I do suspect him. For I learned last night (When the great Duke went to rest) attended by One private follower, he took horse, but whither He's rid, or to what end I cannot guess at, But I will find it out. How the fool stares? What wouldst thou say? Art thou and Ass? Why this; Fopdoodle. And why so sir? This is sport unlooked for. With much formality. Who's that? Very good sir. Would; it is directed: To the Princess Fiorinda. And at your feet too fallen, there's something writ on't.

Giovanni writes to give Lydia advance warning of what he understands to be Cosimo’s dishonourable intentions, and they contrive to have her drunken servant Petronella introduced in her place. Cosimo is duly repelled, and criticizes Carolo for giving her inadequate fatherly guidance. The ruse collapses when Carolo summons the real Lydia. Cosimo has Sannazaro and Giovanni confined and summons the court. Sannazaro manages to get a message to Fiorinda, scratched onto a pane of glass which he drops to her as she arrives. She asks Cosimo to restore him to favour, and agrees to accept him as her husband even after she has heard of his interest in Lydia. Cosimo appoints Lydia and Fiorinda to judge the miscreants, reserving to himself the role of prosecutor. He reminds them that he has sworn not to marry again, and pardons Giovanni and Sannazaro upon ascertaining that Lydia and Fiorinda really do love them.
