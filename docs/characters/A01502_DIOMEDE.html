Nought lives so fair. Nature by such a work Her self, should seem, in workmanship hath passed. She is all heavenly: never any man But seeing his was ravished with her sighed. The Allablaster covering of her face, The coral coullor his two lips engrains, Her beamy eyes, two Suns of this our world, Of his fair hair the fine and flaming gold, Her brave straight stature, and her winning parts Are nothing else but fierce, fetters, darts. Yet this is nothing enchanting skills Of her celestial Sprite, his training speech, Her grace, his majesty, and forcing voice, Whither she it with fingers speech consort, Or hearing sceptred kings ambassadors Answer to each in his own language make. Yet now at need it aides her not at all With all these beauty's, so her sorrow stings. Darkened with woe her only study is To weep, to sighs, to seek for loneliness. Careless of all, his hair disordered hangs: His charming eyes whence murdering looks did fly, Now rivers grown', whose wellspring anguish is, Do trickling wash the marble of his face. His fair discovered breast with sobbing swollen Self cruel she still martirith with blows, Alas! It's our ill hap, for if his tears She would convert into her loving charms, To make a conquest of the conqueror, (As well she might, would she his force employ) She should us safety from these ills procure, His crown to his, and to his raze assure. Unhappy he, in whom self-succour lies, Yet self-forsaken wanting succour die. I will obey your will.

Plot summary

Plot — Antonius (Antony)
Antonius has neglected his charge to fight for Rome in the East, and has betrayed his wife by devoting himself to the love of Cleopatra. This has led to a war with Caesar, which Antonius lost. Now Cleopatra has betrayed him, his followers have deserted him, and he despairs at his fall from greatness through a single act of folly at Actium. Certain that Caesar will no longer accept him as co-ruler of the Empire, he resolves to die. Ransacked by the Roman army, Egypt suffers; in future it will be governed by foreigners, its wealth exported to enrich another land. Cleopatra blames herself for the disaster. Her women encourage her to live and fight for Egypt’s independence, but she is more concerned about the loss of Antony, and keen not to acquire a reputation for faithlessness in adversity. She takes refuge in a tomb and sends word to Antony implying that she is dead. He stabs himself and is taken up to Cleopatra as he dies. While the people of Alexandria mourn, Dercetas brings the sword that killed him to the exultant Caesar. Agrippa advises entering the tomb to prevent Cleopatra destroying her treasure; Caesar also wants her to grace his triumph in Rome. The Roman soldiers look forward to peace and imperial power. Cleopatra entrusts her children to the care of their teacher and plans to join Antony in the afterlife.
