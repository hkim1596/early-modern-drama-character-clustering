To find out you: Who's that, Metellus Cymber ? I am glad on't. What a fearful Night is this? There's two or three of us have seen strange sights. Yes, you are. Oh Cassius, If you could but win the Noble Brutus To our party— All, but Metellus Cymber, and he's gone To seek you at your house. Well, I will High, And so bestow these Papers as you bade me. Oh Caesar. I dreamed to night, that I did feast with Caesar, And things unluckily charge my Fantasy: I have no will to wander forth of doors, Yet something leads me forth. Directly I am going to Caesars Funeral. As a friend. Briefly, I dwell by the Capitol. Truly, my name is Cinna. Oh pardon, Sir, it does; and you gray Lines, That fret the Clouds, are Messengers of Day. Be that the uttermost, and fail not then. Caska, you are the first that rears your hand. Liberty, Freedom; Tyranny is dead, Run hence, proclaim, cry it about the Streets. Here, quite confounded with this mutiny. What is my name? Whither am I going? Where do I dwell? Am I a married man, or a Batchellour? Then to answer every man, directly and briefly, wisely and truly: wisely I say, I am a Bachelor. I am not Cinna the Conspirator. No, by no means.

Plot summary

Plot — Julius Caesar
Caesar returns to Rome triumphant over Pompey. He is warned to beware the Ides of March, and later offered the crown, which he refuses. Meanwhile Cassius sounds out Brutus’ attitude to Caesar’s ambition, and determines to screw him up to conspiracy. Supernatural portents disturb the night before the Ides of March. Brutus convinces himself that Caesar must be assassinated to prevent him establishing himself as a monarch. The conspirators meet at his house, and Brutus insists on limiting the scope of the plot. Cicero is not to be taken into their confidence, Mark Antony is to be spared, and only Caesar murdered. After the conspirators have left, Portia forces Brutus to take her into his confidence. The portents and Calpurnia’s anxiety persuade Caesar to stay at home, but Decius convinces him to go to the Senate as arranged. Artemidorus tries to give him a letter warning against the conspirators, but he refuses to read it. He is stabbed to death, and confusion ensues. Against Cassius’ wishes, Brutus agrees that Antony may speak at Caesar’s funeral. Brutus’ speech justifying the assassination temporarily convinces the mob, but Antony’s rhetoric, and a bequest in Caesar’s will, turns them against the conspirators. Brutus and Cassius flee Rome. In the riots that follow, the conspirators’ houses are burned and Cinna the poet killed, mistaken for the conspirator of the same name. Antony, Octavius, and Lepidus seize power, draw up a death list, and make preparations for war with Brutus and Cassius. Portia commits suicide. Brutus and Cassius quarrel, but reconcile themselves as friends. They determine to give battle at Philippi rather than waiting for Antony and Octavius to come to them at Sardis. Brutus is visited by Caesar’s ghost, which tells him that they will meet again at Philippi. The two armies face off at Philippi, and Cassius, mistakenly thinking that his side is beaten, commits suicide. Once the day really is lost, Brutus has difficulty in finding a friend willing to help him do the same; finally Strato holds his sword while he runs on it and dies. Antony and Octavius, victorious, pay him tribute and promise him an honourable burial.
