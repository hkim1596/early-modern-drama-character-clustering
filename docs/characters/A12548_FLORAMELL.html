The delian Lute is not more Musical Then thy sweere voice, O my Apollo speak, That with the wrapture of thy words, my soul May be entranced, and wish no other joys: That by the discord of two Broken harps, (Old and unfit for Lovers harmony) Our joys should suffer a distate of fear; And in our most delights a qualm of grief Run Like a vain of Led through a Gold-mine. We grow too jealous of our prosperous days, Making and evil, where no ill is meant: Like hallowed ground, love sanctifies this place, And will not suffer danger to intrude. Here we are ringed in earthly Paradise, And may have all the heaven to our selves: Be then Mistrust and exile from my breast, Where lives no jealousy, dwells present rest. And Like the best oblation for your wrath, Lo, I the subject of this variance fallen prostrate on my knee, to suffer death, Ere such a rude act, most vnfather-like, Be put in practice on so good a son. And such is mine to loose thy company. I would, and be inhumed within the ground, Rather then measure out a hated bed. All wet and weary with a boisterous storm, At last I have set my foot upon the Land; I tremble as a Fever shook my oynes, But 'tis the Ocean that has frozen me: Drop there thou moisture of a swelling Flood. And let me see, no Fisherman at hand, To tell me in what Coast I am arrived: Alas I see none, I shall die with cold. You seem to be of a Ma e que state, What should a poor distressed Gentlewoman, Trouble a person of such eminence? I have seen a King ere now, And by your Diadem you should be one: Pray, rather let me die then trouble you. Indeed I am but a poor Gentlewoman, Punished for wearing Jewels in a storm: But I have lost a husband whom I loved, For marrying whom, I have endured this cross; And now his friends, if they should find me out, Would finish what the storm has left undone. It Infortunio, as mine is, With the alteration of a letter only. May the Sea never use you of this fashion; I take your courtesy, and will attend. Does not Idolatrize, beauty's a flower, Which springs and withers almost in and hour: Sickness impairs it, but death kills it quite, It vades as fast as shadows in the night. Why should your Grace call it Divinity? There's nought divine, but that which cannot die. Least I offend by staying here too long, I'll take my leave, and so curb in my tongue. Madame. I am accused, that ought to be excused, And blamed as one unchaste, for being chaste. I Inchant the King, and use Medea's Art? Witchcraft I have always hated with my heart: And except Modesty a Circe be, I know no other kind of Sorcery. Your Highness sent me with a Cup of Wine Unto the King, the occasion of his wooing; Was it my fault to do your Highness will? Judge gracious Majesty but as you ought, And do not blame me for a Virgins trial: His love was answered with a strong denial; And so denied for ever shall he be, That seeks by such means to dishonour me. Before I wrong a Queen so truly kind, I'll mar my face, and make my sad eyes blind. A king say so, pardon me sir, your will I'll not obey. But your oath broken mainly run a away. Exit Floram. Never. The Saint of France forbid it, & all powers, That have continued both so long together In sacred rites of Marriage, heaven deny I should be Author of her Tragedy: Or give content where murder is opposed. Plot summary

Plot — The Hector of Germany, or The Palsgrave, Prime Elector
. Defeated in his attempt to seize power in Spain, Henry the Bastard attempts to get himself elected Holy Roman Emperor; he secures the support of three of the seven Electors, promising that Saxony will be the power behind his throne. But the other Electors, including the sickly Palsgrave, prefer the Duke of Savoy’s candidature, so the Bastard uses force: he defeats the opposing Electors in battle, imprisons two of them, and has himself crowned, then breaks his word and excludes Saxony from power. News of the ===== PAGE 311 ===== 1613 usurpation cures the Palsgrave, who prepares to mobilize an army. Spain is the theatre of war for their first confrontation. The Palsgrave is victorious, and the Bastard is captured, but rescued by Saxony. The warring potentates seek alliances against one another: the Palsgrave goes to England, the Bastard to France. The Palsgrave competes in an English tournament and is installed in the Order of the Garter. King John of France is reluctant to fight the English again (they have too good a track record of victory in France), and hopes to use diplomacy to secure Edward III’s neutrality. Refused the chance to conduct the mission, Saxony goes to England in disguise, but is identified by the Palsgrave, who dishonours him by pulling off a lock of his hair. Diplomacy fails, and Edward and the Palsgrave invade France, dispatching troops to free the imprisoned Electors. The Palsgrave defeats and kills Saxony in single combat. King John withdraws his support from the Bastard, and Savoy is formally crowned Emperor. In the sub-plot, Lord Fitzwaters is betrothed to Floramell, but discovers that she loves his son. He witnesses a secret assignation between the lovers in the garden of Lord Clinton, Floramell’s father. Clinton orders Young Fitzwaters off his land; Lord Fitzwaters goes further and banishes him from England. Floramell goes with him, leaving in her place a cross-dressed page, whom Lord Fitzwaters ‘marries’. The lovers are separated in a shipwreck: Floramell is washed up on the French coast and becomes a maid of honour at court; Young Fitzwaters is marooned on a barren rock and subsists on a fish diet until he is rescued by Saxony. King John is attracted to Floramell and offers to make way for her by having the Queen murdered. Floramell will not consent to adultery or murder, but agrees to give him a single kiss.
