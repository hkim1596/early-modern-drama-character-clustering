But how far distant are we from the Court? It seems to me twenty thousand miles: Yet hope I to be there within this hour. Sweet Gonorill, I long to see thy face, Which hast so kindly gratified my love. Brother of Cambria, we greet you well, As one whom here we little did expect. To say the truth, my Lord, it is no less, And for your partly some hasty wind of chance Hath blown you hither thus upon the sudden. In faith content, and therefore to be brief; For I am sure my haste's as great as yours: I am sent for, to come unto King Leir, Wh by these present letters promises His eldest daughter, lovely Gonorill, To me in marriage, and for present dowry, The moiety of half his Regiment. The Ladies love I long ago possessed: But until now I never had the father's. If I have one half, and you have the other, Then between us we must needs have the whole. Why, the whole Kingdom. IT were pity such rare beauty should be hid Within the compass of a Cloisters wall: But howsoever, if Leirs words prove true, It will be good, my Lord, for me and you. My gracious Lord, and father too, I hope, Pardon, for that I made no greater haste: But were my horse as swift as was my will, I long ere this had seen your Majesty. But you, sweet Love, illustrious Gonorill, The Regent, and the Sovereign of my soul, Is Cornwall welcome to your Excellency? Father, what aileth you to be so sad? Me thinks, you frollike not as you were wont. But if a man can frame himself to mirth, It is a mean for to prolong his life. Comfort your self, father, here comes your daughter, Who much will grieve, I know, to see you sad. My Gonorill, you come in wished time, To put your father from these pensive dumps. In faith, I fear that all things go not well. Sweet, be not angry in a partial cause, He never complained of thee in all his life. Father, you must not weigh a woman's words. I cannot stay to hear this discord found. Ah, Gonorill, what dire unhappy chance Hath sequestered thy father from our presence, That no report can yet be herd of him: Some great unkindness hath been offered him, Exceeding far the bounds of patience: Else all the world shall never me persuade, He would forsake us without notice made. I hope so too; but yet to be more sure, I'll send a Post immediately to know Whither he be arrived there or no. I wonder that the Messenger does stay, Whom we dispatched for Cambria so long since If that his answer do not please us well, And be do show good reason for delay, I'll teach him how to dally with his King, And to detain us in such long suspense. It may be so, and therefore till I know The truth thereof, I will suspend my judgement. From Gallia? what should his message Hither import? is not your father happily Gone thither? well, whatsoever it be, Bid him come in, he shall have audience. What news from Gallia? speak Ambassador. Indeed, my friend, upon some urgent cause, He is at this time absent from the Court: But if a day or two you here repose. 'Tis very likely you shall have him here, Or else have certain notice where he is. Then she is merry, if she have her health. I'll be a mediator in her cause, And seek all means to expiate his wrath. Come in, my friend, and frolic in our Court, Till certain notice of my father come. Presumptuous King of Gawles, how darest thou Presume to enter on our British shore? And more then that, to take our towns perforce, And draw our subjects hearts from their true King? Be suit to buy it at as deer a price, As ere you bought presumption in your lives. The day is lost, our friends do all revolt, And join against us with the adverse partly: There is no means of safety but by flight, And therefore isle to Cornwall with my Queen. It shows thy understanding to be blind, And that thou hadst needs of and interpreter: Well, I will know thy message ere't be long, And find a mean to cross it, if I can.

Plot summary

Plot — The True Chronicle of King Leir (King Lear and his Three Daughters)
King Lear deems it a good moment to marry off his three daughters: with his wife recently dead, they no longer have a mother to direct them. His plan is to retire and divide the kingdom between them as their dowries, and to marry them to neighbouring kings in order to forestall foreign aggression. Cordella, however, is determined to marry for love rather than for diplomatic convenience, so Lear plans to circumvent her by laying on a test: the daughters will make a public declaration of how much they love their father, and he will then ask Cordella to show that love by complying with his wishes over her marriage. Scaliger warns the other daughters, Goneril and Regan, of what Lear has in mind, and misleads them into believing that their shares of the kingdom will reflect the extent of their declared love. Jealous of their younger sister’s beauty and renown, they flatter Lear so that Cordella’s simple statement of due filial love pales in comparison. Lear gives the kingdom wholly to them, summons the Kings of Cornwall and Cambria to be their husbands, and assigns them their shares by lot. Against the advice of the good counsellor Perillus, Lear banishes Cordella from court, and she plans to make her living working as a seamstress. By chance, she meets the King of France, who has come to Britain disguised in search of a wife, and he takes her back to France as his Queen. Lear goes to live with Cornwall and Goneril, but annoys her with his criticisms of her extravagance. Scaliger advises her that halving his allowance will persuade him to take himself off to Regan, and so indeed it does. Cornwall sends a messenger to Regan asking for news of Lear, but Goneril bribes him to deliver instead a libellous letter about her father’s behaviour; she also encourages him to murder Lear. Regan has the same idea, and sets up an opportunity, but at the last moment the messenger is struck by conscience, admits the truth to his intended victim: and runs away.
