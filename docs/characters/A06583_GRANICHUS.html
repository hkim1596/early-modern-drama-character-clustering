Manes, it is pity so proper a man should be cast away upon a Philosopher: but that Diogenes that dog should have Manes that dogbolte, it grieveth nam ture and spiteth art: the one having found the so dis solute, absolute I would say, in body, the other so single singular in mind. I think Diogenes keeps but cold cheer. What then, luck warm? That made Manes run from his master last day. As how? Yes. True. O bade. Ah firs, my master is a king in his parlour for the body, & a God in his study for the soul. Among all his men he commends one that is and excellent Musician, then stand I by and clap another on the shoul der and say, this is a passing good Cook. What dost thou then? But how dost thou then live? Well, for this time I will staunch thy gut, and a mong pots and platters thou shalt see what it is to serve Plato. And other commodity of emptiness. Then stay thou behind, and I will go with Psyllus. Oh Dog. That is easy without thy light to be found, be not all these men? What beast is it thou lookest for? Plot — Campaspe (Alexander, Campaspe, and Diogenes)
King Alexander has conquered Thebes and brought its captives to Athens, where he means to spend a peaceful interlude with the philosophers. Mo~t 1.:on 1 e and debate for him, but Diogenes churlishly refu:;t:"s to comply. Alexander becomes enamoured of Campaspe, and has her painted by Apelles, who himself falls in love with her. Knowing he must not challenge Alexander in love, he creates an excuse to see her again by blemishing the painting so that a second sitting is needed. Diogenes sends his servant to draw a crowd with a report that he will fly, but it is only a pretext to rail at them. Using a false report of a fire in Apelles' shop, Alexander tricks the painter into revealing his feelings for Campaspe, and, establishing that she reciprocates them, graciously withdraws his suit and allows them to marry. Having subdued his own amorous feelings, he will now go to war with Persia and conquer the world.
