Art thou that Fortunatus, whose great name, Being carried in the Chariot of the winds, Has sild the Courts of all our Asian Kings, With love and envy, whose dear presence ties The eyes of admiration to thine eyes? Art thou that Jove that in a shower of gold Appearedst before the Turkish Emperor? Where is that purse which threw abroad such treasure? Most welcome; and most happy are mine arms In circling such and earthly deity: But will not Fortunatus make me blessed By sighed of such a purse? Welcome, most welcome, to the Sultans court; Stay here, and be the King of Babylon, Stay here, and I will more amaze thine eyes With wondrous sights, then can all Asia. Behold you tower, there stands mine Armoury, In which are Corsiets forged of beaten gold, To arm ten hundred thousand fighting men, Whose glittering sqaudrons when the Sun beholds, They seem Like to ten hundred thousand Jove's, When Jove on the proud back of thunder rides, Trapped all in lightning flames: there can I show thee The ball of gold that set all Troy on fire; There shalt thou see the scarf of Cupid's mother, Snatched from the soft moist Ivory of her arm, To wrap about Adonis wounded thigh; There shalt thou see a wheel of Titan's care, Which dropped from heaven when Phaeton fired the world: I'll give Thee (if thou wilt) two silver Doves Composed by Magic to divide the air, Who (as they fly) shall play their silver wings, And give strange music to the Elements, I'll give Thee else the fan of Proserpine, Which in reward (for a sweet Thracian song,) The black-browd Empress three to Mopheus, Being come to fetch Eurydice from hell. Thine shall behold them, and make choice of any, So thou wilt give the Sultan such a purse. Leave us alone: never was mortal ear Acquainted with the virtue of a Jewel, Which now isle show, (out-valuing all the rest.) Behold this Casket,— Draw a Curtain. Fettered in golden chains, the Lock pure gold, The key of sollide gold, which my self keep, And heres the treasure thats contained in it. Takes out the Hat I'll not exchange this, for ten Diadems. On pain of death, none listen to our talk. Oh, yes, for none shall understand the worth Of this inestimable ornament, But you: and yet not you, but that you swear By her white hand, that lent you such a name, To leave a wondrous purse in Babylon. I ; if they do, they die. You see 'tis in show; did I want Jewels, Gold could beget them, but the wide world's wealth Buys not this Hat; this clapped upon my head, I (only with a wish) am through the air, Transported in a moment over Seas, And over lands to any secret place; By this I steal to every Princes court, And hear their private counsels and prevent All dangers which to Babylon are meant. By help of this I oft see armies join, Though when the dreadful Alvarado sounds, I am distant from the place a thousand leagues, O, had I such a purse and such a Hat, The Sultan were, of all, most fortunate. Dead, and the whole world Yields not a workman that can frame the Like. No, 'tis more light then any Hat beside: Your hand shall peise it. Fie, ya're deceived: try it upon your head, Stay, Fortunatus stay, I am undone. Treason, Lords, treason, get me wings, isle fly After this damned Traitor through the air. This Fortunatus, this fend wrongs your king. No gates no grates of Iron imprison him, Like a Magician breaks he through the clouds; Bearing my soul with him, for that Jewellgon, I am dead, and all is dross in Babylon. Fly after him, 'tis vain, on the winds wings, Heel ride through all the Courts of earthly kings. He die that troubles me: call me not king; For isle consume my life in sorrowing. Exeunt.

Plot summary

Plot — Old Fortunatus
Fortunatus gets lost in a wood and falls asleep. He is found by Fortune, attended by fallen kings who complain of her capricious control of their destiny. To spite them, she decides to advance Fortunatus’ fortunes, and offers him a choice of six gifts: wisdom, strength, health, beauty, longevity, or riches. He chooses wealth, and she gives him a purse which will always contain ten gold pieces, of legal tender wherever he may be. Fortunatus travels the world, visiting potentates, and bestows bottomless purses on every host. The Sultan of Babylon boats of his collection of treasures, and shows him the finest: an old hat which will transport its wearer anywhere in an instant. By a ruse, the Sultan puts it on Fortunatus’ head, and he wishes himself back home to Cyprus, thereby acquiring possession of the hat. Fortune decides that he has abused her gifts and reminds him that he did not choose long life or wisdom. He will die at the height of his fortunes with his sins black upon his soul, and will have no opportunity to repent. He dies there and then, leaving the purse and hat to his sons. The virtuous Ampedo takes the hat, while his imprudent younger brother Andelocia has the purse. Andelocia goes to England, where the Prince of Cyprus is wooing the King’s daughter, Agrippine; she is also loved by the melancholy French prisoner Orleans. On arrival, Andelocia is bountiful with largesse, which leaves King Athelstan itching to take him down a peg or two, and eager to learn the source of his riches. When Andelocia plans a magnificent banquet for his hosts, Athelstan orders that nobody is to supply him with any fuel: it will end up being a cold, unappetizing meal. Andelocia dances with Agrippine, and she takes him off to get to know him better. She then drugs him and takes the magic purse as he sleeps, leaving behind a copy. On waking, Andelocia finds that the King’s orders leave him unable to provide the banquet, and the Princess has left him without the wherewithal even to try to pay for it. Andelocia returns home to Cyprus and steals the wishing hat from Ampedo. He acquires some jewels by trickery and returns to England posing as a jeweller. Using the hat, he abducts Agrippine and takes her to a hot country. The climate doesn’t agree with her, so he climbs a tree to pick her a juicy apple; but while he’s up there, she puts on the wishing hat and goes home.
