Let's make up a company of Players, For we can all sing and say, And so (with practice) soon may learn to play. I'll teach ye to play true Politicians. Why proper-fellowes, they play Lords and Kings, Content; Scrivener, hoe, You must tie a knot of Knaves together. Your names he means; the man's learned. I Master Posthaste the Poet. Oh stately Scrivener, thats where dwell ye? Politician Players. Whose but the merry Knight's, sir Oliver Owlets, There was never a better man to Players. What a greasy phrase: This playing will furnish ye. Heeres half a dozen good fellows. The liker to thrive. A speaks to you players; I am the poett. 'Tis not to be dealt on without store of drink. Nay 'tis well said, for drink must clap up the bargain. Lets away. Have you cried the Play, masters? Oh sirs, my wit's grown no less plentiful then the time. Ther's two sheets done in follio, will cost two shillings in time. I that you shall, I swear by the Sunne-sit down sirs, When Authors quill, in quivering hand, His tired arm did take: His wearied Muse, bade him devise, Some fine play for to make. And now my Masters in this brauadoe, I can read no more without Canadoe. Enter the Prodigal Child; fill the pot I would say, Huffa, huffa, who Calais for me? I play the Prodigal child in jollity. Enter to him Dame Virtue: My Son thou art a lost child, (This is a passion, note you the passion?) And hath many poor men of their goods beguiled: Oh prodigal child, and child prodigal. Read the rest sirs, I cannot read for tears, Fill me the pot I prethe fellow Gulch. My masters, what tire wears your lady on her head? Oh amiable good, it's excellent. I'll doo't extempore. Why Lords we are here to show you what we are, Lords we are here although our clothes be bore, In steed of flowers, in season, ye shall gather Rhyme and Reason? I never pleased my self better, it comes off with such suavity. Why man? The glass is run our play is done, Hence Time does call, we thank you all. No matter so they thump us not, Come, come, we poets have the kindest wretches to our Ingles One whose hands are hard as battledores with clap ping at baldness. Content. Why his reward is worth the Mayor & all the town. Sir, we have carowst Like Kings, For here is plenty of all things. God bless my Lord Mavortius, & his merry men all, To make his honour merry, we sing in the hall. We hope (for causes) in the house, though drink be in our heads. Because to Plenty we carouse, for beef and beer, and beds. For mine own partly, though this summer season I am desperate of a horse. A Gentleman's a Gentleman, that hath a clean shirt on, with some learning, and so have I. Mother Gurtons neadle; ( a Tragedy. ) The Devil and Dives; ( a Comedy. ) Arusset coat, and a Knaves cap; ( and Infernal ) A proud heart and a beggars purse; ( a pastoral. ) The Widow's apron-strings; ( a nocturnal. ) My Lords, of your accords, some better pleasure for to bring, if you a theme affords, you shall know it, that I Posthaste the Poet, extempore can sing. Plot summary

Plot — Histriomastix, or The Player Whipped
Under the benevolent rule of Peace, various orders of society turn to learning in their different ways: Mavortius and his fellow noblemen and gentlemen become students of the liberal arts, professional people appoint the scholar Chrisoganus as their lecturer, and some artisans organize themselves as a company of players, Sir Oliver Owlet’s Men. However, the new reign of Plenty tempts many of them away from their studies, leaving only Chrisoganus loyal. While the lawyers and merchants take advantage of the leisure of the vacation to go hunting, Mavortius entertains his guests with a play given by Owlet’s Men. Plenty is succeeded by Pride, who encourages a dedication to fashion and a neglect of proper decorum: the lawyers and merchants enrich themselves unjustly, Mavortius dismisses many of his servants to release more money to spend on pleasure, and both Chrisoganus (who has turned playwright) and Owlet's Men price themselves out of the market. Next, Envy comes to power, causing class resentment in the realm: the citizenry’s adoption of the latest fashions causes resentment among the nobility, envious behaviour disrupts the players’ rehearsal, and Mavortius becomes belligerent. The next ruler is War, under whom the players are conscripted as soldiers, Mavortius fights with his friends, the common people rise in rebellion against their social superiors, and the Spanish invade the realm. Out of this chaos comes Poverty, leading aristocrats, merchants. and peasants alike to debt, bankruptcy, and famine. Owlet’s men, unable to pay their taxes, are banished from the realm. Finally Poverty and her associates are expelled by the return of Peace and Plenty. Queen Elizabeth also appears, in the guise of Astraea, and the play ends with the five lesser rulers resigning their sceptres to Peace.
