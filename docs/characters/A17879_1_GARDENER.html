Full speech

Most magnificent and peerless Diety, lo I the surveyor of Lady Floras works, welcome your grace with fragrant phrases into her Bowers, beseeching your great ness to bear with the late wooden entertainment of the Woodmen, for Woods are more full of weeds then wits, but gardens are weeded, and Gardners witty, as may appear by me. I have flowers for all fancies, Time for truth, Rosemary for remembrance, Roses for love, Hearts ease for joy, and thousands more, which all harmonious lie rejoice at your presence; but my self, with these my Paradisians here, will make you such music, as the wild Wooddists shall be ashamed to hear the report of it. Come sirs prune your pipes, and tune your strings, and a gree together Like birds of a feather. Wonder not (great Goddess) at the sweetness of our Garden-aire (though passing sweet it be) Flora hath perfumed it for you (Flora our mistress, and your servant) who invites you yet further into her Paradise; she inui sibly will lead your grace the way, and we (as our duty is) visibly stay behind. I much joy in thy conversion, thou hast long been a mad fellow, and now proves a good fellow, let us all therefore join together sociably in a Song, to the honour of good fellowship. Silence sirs, here comes Silvanus God of these Woods, whose presence is rare, and imports some no ueltie. I can neither brag of my Travels, nor yet am ashamed of my profession; I make sweet walks for fair Ladies; Flowers I prepare to adorn them; close Arbours I built wherein their Loves unseen may court them; and who can do Ladies better service, or more acceptable? when I was a Child and lay in my Cradle, (a very pretty Child) I remember well that Lady Venus ap peared unto me, and setting a Silver Spade and Rake by my Pillow, bade me prove a Gardener; I told my Mother of it (as became the duty of a good Child) whereupon she provided straight for me two great Platters full of Pap; which having dutifully devoured, I grew to this portraiture you see, sprung fodainely out of my Cabin, and fell to my profession. STay Goddess, stay a little space, Our poor Country love to grace, Since we dare not too long stay you, Accept at our hands, we pray you, These mean presents to express Greater love, then we profess, Or can utter now for woe Of your parting hastened so. Gifts these are, such as were wrought By their hands, that them have brought, Homebred things, which they presumed, After I had them perfumed With my flowery incantation, To give you in presentation At your parting, come feat Lasses With fine curtsies, and smooth faces, Offer up your simple toys To the Mistress of our joys; While we the sad time prolong With a mournful parting song.

Plot summary

Plot — The Royal Entertainment at Caversham
As the Queen arrives at Caversham Park, her way is barred by a woodland Cynic, who wants to be left alone in his happy poverty. He is rebuked by a traveller, who 1613 debates with him the virtues of a poor, solitary life; the Cynic is convinced and renounces it. After the Queen has entered the park, she is welcomed by the keepers with a song. As she approaches the house, the gardeners strive to outdo the woodmen with their song. Further singing welcomes her as she passes into the upper garden. After supper, the Traveller, Gardener, and Cynic return, praise the Queen, and sing. Sylvanus arrives, does homage to the greater goddess visiting the house, and introduces some masquing semi-deities. Dancing ensues, in which the Queen herself participates. The following day, the Queen is to depart. As she is given three home-made presents, the gardeners sing their sorrow at her going.
