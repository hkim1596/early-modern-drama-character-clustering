Sir, here's a gentleman ( Slightall so near?) Desires your conference (no devise at all To shift him hence?) His business craves dispatch, And is of serious urgence; 'twould become Your modesty to spare us some few minutes, Since it no whit concerns you. Son? Pray with draw, it comes from a great man. Daughter, a word. Listen to't, it's all for your preferment. You shall be a great Lady. And take place of all Knights wives in the Shire. His honour? so you shall be honourable, Be every day Caroach'd; and ride in state. But you shall sir, and send his Lordship satisfaction Even to his best desire; tell him from me She shall attend his pleasure: you shall, Nan, Use him with all obsequious reverence, And gladly embrace his motion; Girl, you shall. Forward sir, if you withstand the fortune of your Daughter In this tract of preferment without yours. Send him fair humble language, do my wench Accept his noble pleasure, and return him Courteous and loving thanks; think what it is To be attended, honoured, and advanced. My Lady Anne you shall. How poor and slightly Mistress Slightall sounds? Mistress? thou shalt have such to be thy servants, And curchy to thee when thou turnest thy head; Bow at each nod, and make their Farthingales, At every word thou speakest, to kiss their heals. Footmen, Pages, and your gentlemen Ushers, Walk bore before you. Weel stand to that, let it be put to her. Madam, may it please you take a second choice? You have your answer Slightall, and good sir, Return this to my Lord. Unless your wit were grey as well as your hairs; You grow in years, and so you should in wisdom. Did you never see a bore face teach a beard? It is no news in this so pregnant age. Well, well, man. Why I pray? Because I seek the advancement of our house? One of your gravity? I am ashamed, a Justice by Your place, and can appear so partial to your own; Whom should your providence devise to raise, If not your own? will you be twice a Child? And now, in this your second infancy, Not take care for your Cradle? Why then I am no sheep. It should be then your care to see me better pleased. Think what it is to have a great Lord call you Father in Law, and so your Daughter a great Lady. His Lordship will be here, give him good face, And courteous Language, or I'll make your board As loud as a perpetual Gossips feast, Or a discourse of Fishwives; and your Bed As if you were to lodge in Loth-bury Where they turn brazen Candlesticks. So you are Like to find it. Why well then: Might not this time much better Have been spent, to have taught your Daughter rudiments Of State? her postures, and decorums, fine French Congees, And quaint outlandish Garbs? one that hath been A Courtier in his youth, and brought nought thence? Fie, fie; I am ashamed of't. His Lordship sure? Why such at first, as you must be hereafter; Like a Lady. Proud, but not too perverse; Coy, not disdainful; Strange, but yet not too straight; Like one that would, Were she well wooed, but yet not to be wone, Without some formal Courtship; had it been My case, my wench, when I was young Like thee, Enter Lord Treatwell. I could have born it bravely. See, he's come, Husband, your Duty; Girl, your modest blush, Mixed with a kind of strange, but loving welcome; Were I as young as once: Your Lordship Sir, Hath done my House much honour. You see sir, what homely entertainment, And how course our poor House can afford you. What says my Child? He that kissed you last. Minion, how? Partly of it lies in what he left behind, Observance, state, retinue, and attendance, Of which you must partake. How cast thou fool? his Nobility lies in his blood, His Blood? Art thou mad? Where I was wont to call thee baggage, Nan, And paltry Girl, I must not dare to speak to your Honour, without a Prologue of some half and hour long, Which must begin, an't please your Ladyship. Dost thou not see him there? A good riddance. There's no affright in this. Wondrously. Ten days, no more, Partly of that time that Slightall hath been Mad, My daughter passed her senses. No, only one by Chamber stands remote, And in that after Midnight there's a noise, That troubles the whole house of howlings, shrickes, Fireworks and Crackers, as if the great squib-maker Were new come from beyond Sea. Only my desperate Husband, who, one night, Would be so foolish venturous as lodge there, But did return so frighted, told such wonders, Still fright me to remember. Heaven rest her soul, my mother. A Devil sir Like me? Here's now my Husband, he can speak it all. And now art found. Made me again thy Mother, and this work, Your ever patient and obedient wife. Sick in her Chamber, where she keeps her Bed, And dares not thence remove.

Plot summary

Plot — A New Trick to Cheat the Devil
Anne Changeable wants to marry Slightall, with her father’s consent, but her mother persuades her that marriage to Lord Scales will be better for her social standing. Slightall takes rejection badly: he turns to misogyny and voluptuous hedonism. One of his servants, Roger, tries unsuccessfully to dissuade him from squandering his money, while the other, Geoffrey, helps make an arrangement with a usurer to supply him with a further £1,000 — the usurer’s objective being to acquire his land. Lord Scales visits Anne, and she finds that he is not a good kisser: Slightall is the better catch in every respect save his lack of a title; but when she tries to reactivate the romance, Slightall rejects her. He has been ruined by prodigality and pays off his servants; Geoffrey is taken on by Lord Scales, but Roger declines a similar offer. Anne insults everyone who persuaded her to jilt Slightall, but this only makes Lord Scales more attracted to her. Changeable promises Anne that he will get Slightall back for her. Driven out of his wits with melancholy, Slightall conjures up Beelzebub, and makes a contract: the devil will give him enough money to pay his debts in exchange for the right to take his soul when he no longer owes anyone anything. On the day his debt to the usurer falls due, he turns out to have ample money to pay, so the usurer gets his money back rather than the lands he coveted. Asked by Anne to give Slightall some financial support, Lord Scales finds his offer rejected with contempt. The Changeables’ house is troubled by a female spirit; Slightall agrees to help exorcise it by spending the night in the haunted room. When he does so, he is visited by the ghost in Anne’s form. Beelzebub comes for Slightall’s soul, but Slightall demonstrates that he is not yet clear of debt: he paid the usurer with money loaned by Beelzebub. The devil accepts the situation and offers to marry him to the she-devil who haunts the Changeables’ house. Anne’s mother and Lord Scales give their consent, and watch as a friar performs the ceremony. In fact it is not a ghost in Anne’s form, but Anne herself; and ‘Beelzebub’ was really Changeable: Anne has finally got her man back. In the sub-plot, two friars are detained until late by charitable visitation, and find themselves locked out of their abbey. They ask Nan to give them a bed for the night.
