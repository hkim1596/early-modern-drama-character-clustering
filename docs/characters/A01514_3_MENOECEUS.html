You rather ought, Oh father, to consent Unto my death, since that my death may bring No greater honour than to die for thy country. Unto this town both peace and victory. "Ne can I purchase more praise worthy death "Than for my countries wealth to loose my breath "You know deer father, that this life of ours "Is brittle, short, and nothing else in deed "But tedious toil and pangs of endless pain: "And death, whose dart to some men seems so fell, Death (indeed) yieldeth more pleasure than life. "Brings quiet end to this unquiet life. "Unto which end who soonest does arrive, "Fi S soonest rest of all his restless grief. "And were it so, that here on earth we felt "No prick of pain, nor that our flattering days "Were never dashed by froward Fortune's frown, "Yet being born (as all men are) to die, "Were not this worthy glory and renown, "To yield the country soil where I was born, "For so long time, so short a time as mine? I can not think that this can be denied. Then if to shun this haughty high behest, Mine only cause, Oh father, does you move, Be sure, you seek to take from me your son, The greatest honour that I can attain: But if your own commodity you move, So much the less you ought the same allow: For look, how much the more you have in Thebes, So much the more you ought to love the same: Here have you Hemone, he that in my stead (Oh my deer father) may with you remain, So that, although you be deprived of me Yet shall you not be quite deprived of heirs. Although I, father, ought obey your bests, Yet evil it were in this to yield your will. O, tender pity moves me thereto. Nay them to obey, that will I shall not live. But God it seeth that every secret seeth. We know it then, when he reveals the same. By diverse means his secrets he discloseth. You may of me, as of your self dispose, And since my life does seem so deer to you, I will preserve the same to your avail, That I may spend it always to your will. And where, or in what place shall I become? You may command, and I ought to obey. Where Dodona does fit in sacred chair? And who shall guide my wandering steps? Who shall give sustenance for my relief? But when shall I eftesoones my father see? First would I take my congé of the Queen, That since the day my mother lost his life, Hath nourished me as if I were his own. Behold father, I go. You dams of Thebes, Pray to almighty Jove for my return: You see how mine unhappy stars me driven To go my country from: and if so chance, I end in woe my prime and lusty years Before the course of Nature do them call, Honour my death yet with your dreary plaints: And I shall eke, where so this carcase come, Pray to the Gods that they preserve this town. Yet worthy were not that unworthy change. Not you, but me, the heavens call to die. I father ought, so ought not you, to die. Yet wise is he, that does obey the Gods Whose life they take, they give him life also. Who lives (father) here without a fault?
