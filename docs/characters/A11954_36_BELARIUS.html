A goodly day, not to keep house with such, Whose Roof's as low as ours: Sleep Boys, this gate Instructs you how t'adore the Heavens; and bows you To a morning's holy office. The Gates of Monarchs Are Arched so high, that Giants may et through And keep their impious Turbonds on, without Good morrow to the Sun. Hail thou fair Heaven, We house in the Rock, yet use thee not so hardly As prouder livers do. How you speak. Did you but know the Cities Usuries, And felt them knowingly: the Art of the Court, As hard to leave, as keep: whose top to climb Is certain falling: or so slippery, that The fear's as bade as falling. The toil of the War, A pain that only seems to seek out danger In the name of Fame and Honour, which die in the search, And hath as oft a slanderous Epitaph, As Record of fair Act. Nay, many times Does ill deserve, by doing well: what's worse Must curtsy at the Censure. O Boys, this Story The World may read in me: My body's marked With Roman Swords; and my report, was once First, with the best of Note. Cymbeline loved me, And when a Soldier was the Theme, my name Was not far off: then was I as a Tree Whose boughs did bend with fruit. But in one night, A Storm, or Robbery (call it what you will) Shook down my mellow hangings: nay my Leaves, And left me bore to weather. My fault being nothing (as I have told you oft) But that two Villains, whose false Oaths prevailed Before my perfect Honour, swore to Cymbeline, I was Confederate with the Romans: so Followed my Banishment, and this twenty years, This Rock, and these Demesnes, have be my World, Where I have lived at honest freedom, paid More pious debts to Heaven, then in all The fore-end of my time. But, up to the Mountains, This is not Hunter's Language; he that strikes The Venison first, shall be the Lord of the Feast, To him the other two shall minister, And we will fear no poison, which attends In place of greater State: I'll meet you in the Valleys. How hard it is to hide the sparks of Nature? These Boys know little they are Sons to the King, Nor Cymbeline dreams that they are alive. They think they are mine, And though trained up thus meanly In the Cave, whereon the Bow their thoughts do hit, The Roofs of Palaces, and Nature prompts them In simple and low things, to Prince it, much Beyond the trick of others. Jove, When on my three-foot stool I fit, and tell The warlike feats I have done, his spirits fly out Into my Story: say thus mine Enemy fell, And thus I set my foot on's neck, even then The Princely blood flows in his Cheek, he sweats, Strains his young Nerves, and puts himself in posture That acts my words. The younger Brother Cadwall, Once Arviragus, in as Like a figure Strikes life into my speech, and shows much more His own conceiving. Plot summary

Plot — Cymbeline, King of Britain
Imogen has been the heir to Cymbeline’s kingdom since the disappearance of her infant brothers twenty years ago. Now her father intends her to marry her foolish stepbrother Cloten; but she pre-empts this by marrying Posthumus instead. Cymbeline objects to Posthumus, whom he took in as an orphan and brought up at court, and banishes him. The Queen pretends support for the couple, but is really the source of Cymbeline’s hostility; her objective is to bring the succession into her own family line, if necessary by destroying Imogen. Claiming to be studying toxicology, she asks Cornelius to give her some poison for experimental purposes. He mistrusts her motives: the ‘poison’ he supplies is really only a sleeping potion. She then gives it to Posthumus’ servant, Pisanio, claiming it to be a powerful medicine. Posthumus goes to Rome, leaving his bracelet behind with Imogen as a love-token, and taking her ring. There he boasts of his wife’s beauty and chastity, and bets Iachimo the ring that she will never succumb to his seduction. Iachimo comes to Britain, tells Imogen that Posthumus is behaving adulterously in Rome, and tries to persuade her to do the same. When she refuses, he claims he was only testing her, and asks her to take charge of a chest of valuables, partly paid for by Posthumus, and lodge them for the night in a safe place, her bedroom. He hides in the chest and emerges when she is asleep, giving him the opportunity to inspect the room, look at her in bed, and take Posthumus’ bracelet from her arm. Back in Rome, he claims to have enjoyed Imogen sexually, and the bracelet convinces Posthumus that he is telling the truth. Iachimo takes the ring and Posthumus sends two letters home: one to Imogen asking her to meet him at Milford Haven, the other to Pisanio ordering him to kill her on arrival there. Pisanio escorts her as planned, but then gives her boy’s clothes to enable her to escape from Britain; he also passes on the ‘medicine’ he had from the Queen, then writes to tell Posthumus that she has been killed as ordered. Cymbeline has neglected to pay the tribute to Rome agreed by his predecessors. Caius Lucius comes to demand it, but gets a dusty answer. Rome prepares its 1610 forces for an invasion. Imogen is found to be absent from court, and, stung by her contemptuous rejection, Cloten follows her wearing a suit of Posthumus’ clothes, intending to rape her and bring her back humiliated to court.
