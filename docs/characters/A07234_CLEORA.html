No surely, I will not cheapen any of their Wares, Till you have made your Market: you will buy I know at any rate. Enter Timagor. Leosthe nes, Asotus, Timoleon in black, led in by Archid. Diphilus, Cleon. followed by Pisander, Gracculo, Cymbrio, and others. The less your honour. If a Virgin, Whose speech was ever yet ushered with fear, One knowing modesty, and humble silence To be the choicest ornaments of our sex, In the presence of so many Reverend men, Struck dumb with terror and astonishment, Presume to cloth her thought in vocal sounds, Let her find pardon. First, to you, great Sir, A bashful Maid's thanks, and her zealous prayers Winged with pure innocence, bearing them to Heaven, For all prosperity, that the Gods can give To one, whose piety must exact their care, Thus low I offer. Then thus to you My noble Father, and these Lords, to whom I next owe duty, no respect forgotten To you my Brother, and these bold young men (Such I would have them) that are, or should be The Cities Sword and Target of defence. To all of you, I speak; and if a blush Steal on my cheeks, it is shown to reprove Your paleness; willingly I would not say Your cowardice, or fear: think you all treasure Hid in the bowels of the Earth, or Shipwrecked In Neptune's watery Kingdom, can hold weight When Liberty, and Honour, fill one scale, Triumphant Justice sitting on the beam? Or dare you but imagine that your gold is Too deer a salary for such as hazard Their blood, and lives in your defence? For me And ignorant Girl, bear witness heaven, so far I prize a Soldier, that to give him pay With such Devotion as our Flamens Offer Their Sacrifices at the holy Altar, I do lay down these jewels, will make sale Of my superfluous Wardrobe to supply The meanest of their wants. How! your Slaves? Oh stain of Honour! once more, Sir, your pardon, And to their shames, let me deliver, what I know in justice you may speak. Are you men? (For Age may qualify, though not excuse The backwardness of these) able Young men? Yet now your Countries libertie's at the stake, Honour, and glorious triumph, made the garland For such as dare deserve them; a rich Feast Prepared by Victory of immortal vyands, Not for base men, but such as with their Swords Dare force admittance, and will be her Guests, And can you coldly suffer such rewards To be proposed, to Labourers and Slaves? While you that are born Noble (to whom these Valued at their best rate, are next to Horses, Or other Beasts of carriage) cry aim, Like idle lookers on, till their proud worth Make them become your Master's? No; far, far be it from you, Let these of meaner quality contend, Who can endure most labour; Plough the earth, And think they are rewarded, when their sweat Brings home a fruitful Harvest to their Lords; Let them prove good Artificers, and serve you For use and ornament, but not presume To touch at what is Noble; if you think them Unworthy to taste of those Cates you feed on, Or wear such costly garments; will you grant them The privilege and prerogative of great minds, Which you were born to? Honour, won in war And to be styled preservers of their Country Are Titles fit for free and generous Spirits, And not for Bondmen: had I been born a man And such never dying glories made the prize To bold Heroic Courage; By Diana, I would not to my Brother, nay my Father, Be bribed to partly with the least piece of honour I should gain in this Actium. It's and honour: Gives her Scarf. And so I do receive it. And still continue ignorant, For I must be most cruel to my self, If I should teach you. May it not burn here, And as a Seamark, serve to guide true Lovers, (Tossed on the Ocean of luxurious wishes) Safe from the rocks of Lust into the harbour, Of pure affection? rising up and example, Which aftertimes shall witness, to our glory, First took from us beginning. Alas, I then was witty To plead against my self, and mine eye fixed, Upon the hill of Honour, never descended To look into the vail of certain dangers, Through which, you were to cut your passage to it. No, that must not be, For so to serve my own ends, and to gain A petty wreathe my self; I rob you of A certain triumph, which must fallen upon you, Or Virtue's turned a handmaid to blind Fortune: How is my soul divided! to confirm you, In the opinion of the world, most worthy To be beloved, (with me you are at the heighth, And can advance no further) I must send you To Court the Goddess of stern War, who if She see you with my eyes, will never return you, But grow enamoured of you. Where? This is strange! Can you think, I may be tempted? And 'twas That modesty that took me, and preserves me, Like a fresh Rose, in mine own natural sweetness; Which sullied with the touch of impute hands, Lose both sent and beauty. Will you then confirm, That love, and jealousy, though of different nature's, Must of necessity be twins? the younger, Created only to defeat the elder, And spoil him of his Birthright: it's not well. But being to partly, I will not chide, I will not, Nor with one sillable, or tear express, How deeply I am wounded with the arrows Of your distrust: but when that you shall hear At your return, how I have born my self, And what and auftere penance I take on me, To satisfy your doubts: when Like a Vestal I show you to your shame, the fire still burning, Committed to my charge by true affection, The people joining with you in the wonder. When by the glorious splendour of my sufferings, The prying eyes of jealousy are struck blind, The Monster too that feeds on fears, even starved For want of seeming matter to accuse me, Expect Leosthenes, a sharp reproof From my joust anger. Obey me, Or from this minute you are a stranger to me. And do it without reply: all seeing Sun, Thou witness of my innocence, thus I close Mine eyes against thy comfortable light, Till the return of this distrustful man. Now bind they sure, nay doo't, if uncompelled. I lose this knot, until the hands that made it Be pleased to untie it, may consuming plagues Fallen heavy on me, pray you guide me to your lips, This kiss, when you come back shall be a Virgin To bid you welcome: Nay, I have not done yet. It needs not, Sir, I prize him to his worth, Nay, love him truly, yet would not live slaved To his jealous humours. Since by the Hope's of heaven, As I am free from violence, in a thought I am not guilty. May that move you To pity poor Marullo. I am much distracted; in Leosthenes I can find nothing justly to accuse, But his excess of love, which I have studied To cure with more then common means, yet still It grows upon him. And if I may call My sufferings merit, I stand bound to think on Marullos dangers; though I save his life, His love is unrewarded: I confess, Both have deserved me, yet of force must be Unjust to one; such is my destiny. Plot summary

Plot — The Bondman (The Noble Bondman)
Carthage has territorial designs on Sicily, so Corinth sends its general, Timoleon, to take charge of the defence of Syracuse. On arrival, he criticizes the Sicilians for appointing unworthy and inexperienced senators and for general corruption. He decrees that all private wealth should be sequestered to pay for the war. It is an unpopular measure until Cleora public- spiritedly gives up her jewels, forcing the others to follow her example. Cleora has several suitors, including Pisander of Thebes; but her brother Timagoras, keen for her to marry his friend Leosthenes, slandered Pisander to induce their father to send him away. Now that the young men are going to war, Leosthenes is jealous about what she may get up to in his absence, so she promises literally not to see or say anything until his return. The slaves of Syracuse take advantage of the absence of the army and rebel, enslaving their former masters. The ringleader is Cleora’s slave Marullo, who claims to be motivated by unrequited passion for his mistress, but, when he gains access to her, disclaims any intention of raping her and receives in return a wordless acknowledgement of her regard for him. He encourages the former slaves to treat their former masters well, since their positions may again be reversed. News arrives of a victory over the Carthaginians, with particular military distinction achieved by Leosthenes. The rebellious slaves man the city’s walls and gates to keep out the returning army; from the walls, Marullo declares their grievances and demands their freedom. Timoleon realizes they will have to fight their way into the city, but the slaves put up a valiant resistance until cowed by the sight of their masters’ whips. Marullo flees to Cleora’s house, where he is captured. Cleora tells Leosthenes what happened, and asks him to use his influence to have Marullo spared, but this only makes Leosthenes jealous again. Cleora’s father gives her freedom to choose her own husband; he respects Marullo for his restraint in not ransacking the house during the revolt. Cleora realizes she must marry either Leosthenes or Marullo: both are worthy of her, but she is bound to disappoint one. On hearing that Marullo has been ill-treated by his captors, she rushes off to visit him in prison. Leosthenes and Timagoras follow, alerted by her maid, and find them alone together.
