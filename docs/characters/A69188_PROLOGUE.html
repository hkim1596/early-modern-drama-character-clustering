Pardon me sir, my office is to speak a Prologue, not to provide you stools. A mere stranger sir. None of the three ways I assure you. Not on his knees in a comer to Apollo, praying that his play may hold in a good hand at Passage, nor on the stage amongst Gall lants, preparing a bespoke Plaudite; but close in his study writing hard, to get him a handsome suit against Summer. None I protest Do Poets use to bespeak their Auditory? Then must our Author looké for a certain disgrace, for he is altogether unfurnished of such a friendly audience. Not out of any dogged disposition, nor that it figures any certain state, or private government: far be that supposition from the thought of any indifferent Auditor: and the argument being a little string or ivolet, drawn from the full strain of the right worthy Gentleman Sir Philip Sydnies well known Arcam Diamond, confirms it: only a Duke, to make trial of certain experi ments, retires with his retinue into a nameless desert. Now as well for fashion sake, as that all those which have to do in that de sert, are gulled in the reach of their Hope's; therefore he calls it, (and as he presumest, not improperly) The I'll of Gulls. None I protest sir, only in the person of Dametas, he ex presses to the life the monstrous and deformed shape of vice, aswell to beget a loathing of abuse, as that his villainy may give the greater luster to the virtuous dispositions of trueborn gentility. Chaste ears would never endure it fir. Alas Gentlemen, bow is't possible to content you? you will have railing, and invectives, which our Author neither dares, nor affects: you baudy and scurrill jests, which neither becomes his modesty to write, nor the ear of a generous Auditory to hear: you must have swelling comparisons, and um ast Epithets, which are as fit for the body of a Come edy as Her less shoe for the foot of a Pigmey: yet all these we must have, and all in one play, or 'tis already condemned to the hell of eternal disgrace. Nay I beseech you sir, if you be his friend, stand so to him still, for he hath too many enemies already, in whose judgements, he and his labours stand excommunicate, as though unworthy to present themselves in this assembly. Either see it all or none; for 'tis grown into a custom at plays, if any one rise (especially of any fashionable sort) about what serious business soever, the rest thinking it in dislike of the play, though he never thinks it, cry mew, by esus vild; and leave the poor heartless children to speak their Epilogue to the empty seats. You should not deal gentlemanlike with us else.
