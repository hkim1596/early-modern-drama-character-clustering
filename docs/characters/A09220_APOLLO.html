Full speech

So beauty hight the wrack of Priam's Troy. Venus, that fauou is denied him flatly, He is a man and therefore by out laws, Him self, without his aid, must plead his cause. But, Madam, fits it majesty divine, In any sort from justice to decline? And Juno, I with them, and they with me, In law and right, must needfully agree: His pain, his pain, his never dying pain, A cause to make to many more complain. From Ida woods now wends the shepherd's boy, That in his bosom caries fire to Troy. Thrice reverend gods, and thou immortal Jove. If Phœbus may, as him does much behoove, Be licensed, according to our Laws, To speak uprightly in this doubted cause, (Sith women's wits work men's unceasing woes) To make them Friends, that now been frendles foes, And peace to keep with them, with us, and all That make their title to this golden ball: (Nor think ye gods my speech does derogate From sacred pour of this immortal senate,) Refer this sentence where it does belong, In this say I fair Phoebe hath the wrong. Not that (I mean) her beauty bears the prize: But that the holly Law of heaven denies, One God to meddle in and others pour. And this befell so near Diana bower, As for thappeazinge this unpleasant grudge, (In my conceit) she hight the fittest judge. If Jove comptrol not Pluto's hell with charms, If Mars have souraigne pour to manage arms: If Bacchus bear no rule in Neptune sea Nor Vulcan's fire doth Saturn's sith obey: Suppress not then, against law and equity, Diana's power in her own territory: Whose regiment, amid her sacred oars, As proper height as any rule of yours. Well may we so wipe all the speech away, That Pallas, Juno, Venus hath to say, And answer that by justice of our laws, We were not suffered to conclude the cause. And this to me most equal doom appears, A woman to be judge among her pheeres.

Plot summary

Plot — The Arraignment of Paris
Minor deities greet Juno, Pallas, and Venus and present them with gifts. Intending Troy’s downfall, Ate leaves a golden apple where the three goddesses will find it. Seeing that it is addressed to the most beautiful, they quarrel about which of them should be the recipient, then refer the case to the judgement of the next passer-by. This proves to be Paris, who has just pledged his love to Oenone. Each goddess offers him a reward for judging in her favour: Juno offers wealth, Pallas fame, and Venus love, in the person of Helen. Paris chooses Venus, and wins the enmity of the other two. Colin the shepherd dies for love of Thestylis. Not knowing that he has jilted Oenone, Venus tells Paris of the unpleasant consequences for lovers who break their vows, and punishes Thestylis with unrequited love for an ugly old man. Mercury summons Paris to the court of Jove, to answer charges of partiality laid by Juno and Pallas. He pleads his case before the Council of Gods: the court acknowledges its justice and sends him home to the doomed city of Troy. Juno and Pallas appeal, and Venus gives up the golden apple, confident that it will be awarded to her a second time. At Apollo’s suggestion, Diana is appointed judge in the case, since the incident originally happened near her bower. Diana awards the golden apple to the nymph Eliza, ruler of Elysium (representing Queen Elizabeth, in the audience). The goddesses of destiny, according to their annual custom, also resign their symbols of power to the Queen.
