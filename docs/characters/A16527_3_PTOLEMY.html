Might not the Macedonians all b'asham'd, If rendered vassals thus t'a barbarous brood? What? should we bear the yoke that we have framed, To buy disgrace have we bestowed our blood. Our ancestors whose glory we obscured, Would get some vantage of their Nephews thus: They warrd that peoples wrack to have procured, And have we ward to make them Lords over us? Ah, bury this as a'xecrable thing, And let this purpose be no more pursued, For though they were begotten by our king, Yet were they born of those that we subdued. Obraue Leonides, I Like thy strife, That with so few perforrnd so glorious things: And death preferred before th'infamous life, That bondage still from a Barbarian brings. Those loathed t'accept a stranger for their Lord, And with their blood gave flame t'an unknown field, Yet we would honour them that they abhorred, And though being victor's to the vanquished yield. Was this the scope of all our conquersts then, Of our own captives to be made the prey? No, let us still command Like valorous men, And rule our Empire by some other way. May we not use this policy a space, Till better wits some better means devise? Lest dangerous discords do disturb our peace, Still when we would of serious things advice. Let a majestic Senate gathered be, And them amongst the Imperial chare of state: That of authority all signs may see, Then while we compass that respected seat. There those that were in credit with the king, Whose merits in men's minds have reverence bred: Shall in their judgements balance every thing, How kingdoms should be ruled, how Armies led. And what the greatest partly hath once approved, To that the rest must obliged be t'incline: All Army by this harmony being moved, Will execute what ever we design. This concord would prove happy for us all, Since it each state in greatest surety renders: And by this means our Macedony shall, In place of one have many Alexanders. None needs to wonder much though we negle One whose election might procure our shame: His mother's baseness Justice might object, Whom bastardy secludes from such a claim. But yet had nature purged the spot she made, We with his birth the better might comport: While fatherlike in all affairs he had, Given proof of parts that might the state support. He falsifies his raze of wit so weak, That all his inward wants are soon perceived: All of his judgement in derision speak, By which great things can hardly be conceived. And though his body might from pains be sparde, Whose constitution is not very strong, But with infirmities so far impairde That it aline cannot continue long: Yet since in state he never hath been schooled, His ignorance would rack him still with fears: While he that ruled still needing to be ruled, Spoke but with others tongues, herd with their ears. A inconstant king great confusion makes, Whom all mistrust, and most amid a Camp: While soft Like wax, he each impression takes, And does for frivolous things still change the stamp. Ah, should our lives depend upon his breath, That of himself cannot discern a crime: But dooms by informations men to death, Then barren pity yields when out of time. Thus while some always must his judgement sway, That still does harbour in another's head: Of Sycophants this prince may be the prey, That where they list, th'authority will lead. And being but base, that they may be the best, Such still will toil that we may be overthrown: And sometime may the credulous king suggest, To taint our fame, lest it obscure their own. Plot summary

Plot — The Alexandraean Tragedy
Juno remains angry that a Trojan adjudged Venus the most beautiful goddess. Through the centuries, she has sought to avenge herself by harrying the Trojans’ 1607 descendants, the Romans, most recently by fomenting a civil war. But the victor of that war was Caesar, who traces his descent from Venus; he has conquered much of the world and established his ascendancy in Rome, but Juno now intends to work his overthrow. Caesar explains to Antonius that he wants to be a king not for personal ambition but to comply with a Sibylline prophecy which makes monarchy a precondition for a Roman victory in the east. Antonius warns him that there are Romans who desire his death. Cicero laments the loss of Rome’s liberty, and Decius Brutus agrees that, though Caesar’s past actions have been honourable, he should have demobilized his armies now that there is no further external threat. Brutus and Cassius agree that they will not participate in any ceremony aimed at making Caesar a king. Brutus is Caesar’s friend, but reasons that he must assassinate the tyrant for the public good. His wife Portia wrings the secret of the conspiracy out of him. Brutus and Cassius recruit conspirators, both patriots and men who have a personal grudge against Caesar. However, they exclude Cicero and agree not to kill Antonius. Caesar’s wife Calpurnia tries to persuade him not to go to the Senate on the Ides of March — an astrologer has foretold his death, and she herself has had bad dreams - but Decius Brutus induces him to change his mind. The conspirators assassinate Caesar. Antonius makes a show of friendship with them, but is obviously planning reprisals. A messenger brings Caesar’s wife Calpurnia a full account of the circumstances of the murder.
