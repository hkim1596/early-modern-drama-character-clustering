This the Mistress? I make bold, And Like a rude intruder, press upon you Sudden, and unawares. Sir, I desire your more familiar love, Whom I shall study hereafter to respect According to your worth and gravity. Mrs. Anne, for so your name was given me. Were it base, But it's much better then I yet deserve, That face alone would make and dignify't; Your Welcome I approve, good sir, a word: Nay, Master Treatwell, you may witness it, We do not trade in secrets. And that's all I can demand. There's no condition you have yet proposed But warrants grant, they are so reasonable. Mistress Anne, it were superfluous to begin a Suit Which hath before been entered; and I know Made known to you ere this; I come not now For motion, but for answer: All those honours Titles, and Dignities conferred on me We likewise do communicate with you. Court Ladies I have often seen and tried, Fair Country Damsels, Virgins of choice beauty, Bred from the City Scarlet, and among these I might have made my choice, but all relinquished To place affection here; what comfort Lady? Why no such thing. Why Ceremony; The gift of Princes, and the pride of States, Regard in the Weal public, and employment Respect, and duty. I am he. Why, I entreat you? I am so. It is confessed. To whom I pray? You trifle with me. Slightall quoth a? if she slight all as she hath slighted me, Shee'le fright hence all her Suitors. I'll take your kindness, He's no good Soldier that at first repulse Will leave the Breach; belike she's fasting now, I'll take her in full stomach. My Rival so dejected? And what's thy Suit? Thou shalt have't See, Master Treatwell, that his name b'inrold Among my other Servants; let my Steward Receiye such notice from you. What fellow's that? And will he serve? Wil't thou depend on me? Thy reason friend? Suffer this? Is he such? O Master Changeable, how is't with your Daughter? This his servant, since entertained by me hath told me all. But Mistress Changeable, why did you call me these? I pray you do. Nay good sir spare your tears, She hath hit us all alike; this her ingeniousness Adds to her beauty, not detracts at all; I love her never the worse, nor any here Whom her discourse hath touched: it's witty frenzy, And no malicious cancor; so I take it: Nay grieve not you good woman, whom ever long I hope to Title Mother; doubt it not, all shall be well. Let not that stick in thy stomach, never couldst thou Light on a more fair and sweet Godmother, To give thee a name; I'll have all friends, let's in, And comfort the sad Gentleman, and after to supper. Leaving that; I understand you are a Gentleman Nobly derived; but withal, you're poor. Who should buy't? He's mad still, leave him. Stay, first we will debate in friendly terms, Before you grow to outrage. I'll be brief, I understood your state was much Decayed, which in my honour I commiserating, My purpose was in some sort to relieve you; And would you better understand your self, It might appear a noble courtesy. Because it came so free and uncompelled Counsail'd well: there's twenty pieces for you. This saucy fellow usurps upon our patience, Which we'll punish be our strong hand. He braved necessity, and outfaced want, And took my proffered largess in such scorn, As he had been some great Kings Treasurer; My bounty he defied with shaking pockets, The noise whereof deafened and seemed to drown The found of my despised Charity; Some pieces I would willingly have given. Plot summary

Plot — A New Trick to Cheat the Devil
Anne Changeable wants to marry Slightall, with her father’s consent, but her mother persuades her that marriage to Lord Scales will be better for her social standing. Slightall takes rejection badly: he turns to misogyny and voluptuous hedonism. One of his servants, Roger, tries unsuccessfully to dissuade him from squandering his money, while the other, Geoffrey, helps make an arrangement with a usurer to supply him with a further £1,000 — the usurer’s objective being to acquire his land. Lord Scales visits Anne, and she finds that he is not a good kisser: Slightall is the better catch in every respect save his lack of a title; but when she tries to reactivate the romance, Slightall rejects her. He has been ruined by prodigality and pays off his servants; Geoffrey is taken on by Lord Scales, but Roger declines a similar offer. Anne insults everyone who persuaded her to jilt Slightall, but this only makes Lord Scales more attracted to her. Changeable promises Anne that he will get Slightall back for her. Driven out of his wits with melancholy, Slightall conjures up Beelzebub, and makes a contract: the devil will give him enough money to pay his debts in exchange for the right to take his soul when he no longer owes anyone anything. On the day his debt to the usurer falls due, he turns out to have ample money to pay, so the usurer gets his money back rather than the lands he coveted. Asked by Anne to give Slightall some financial support, Lord Scales finds his offer rejected with contempt. The Changeables’ house is troubled by a female spirit; Slightall agrees to help exorcise it by spending the night in the haunted room. When he does so, he is visited by the ghost in Anne’s form. Beelzebub comes for Slightall’s soul, but Slightall demonstrates that he is not yet clear of debt: he paid the usurer with money loaned by Beelzebub. The devil accepts the situation and offers to marry him to the she-devil who haunts the Changeables’ house. Anne’s mother and Lord Scales give their consent, and watch as a friar performs the ceremony. In fact it is not a ghost in Anne’s form, but Anne herself; and ‘Beelzebub’ was really Changeable: Anne has finally got her man back. In the sub-plot, two friars are detained until late by charitable visitation, and find themselves locked out of their abbey. They ask Nan to give them a bed for the night.
